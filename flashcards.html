Management of Care

1.What does the Patient Self-Determination Act of 1990 require of health care facilities?

Facilities receiving Medicare or Medicaid funds must ask every adult client on admission whether they have an advance directive, give written information about their right to make one, and document the answer in the record. Care cannot be conditioned on whether a directive exists.

2.What is the difference between a living will and a durable power of attorney for health care?

A living will states in writing which life-sustaining treatments the client wants or refuses if unable to speak for themselves. A durable power of attorney for health care names a proxy who makes medical decisions on the client's behalf once the client loses decision-making capacity.

3.A client with a DNR order develops chest pain. Should the nurse still treat it?

Yes. A do-not-resuscitate order only means no CPR or intubation if the heart or breathing stops. The nurse still assesses the client, gives oxygen, analgesics, nitrates, and all other ordered treatments, because DNR does not mean do not treat.

4.What is a POLST form and how does it differ from an advance directive?

A POLST, or Portable Medical Orders for Life-Sustaining Treatment, is a set of actionable medical orders signed by a provider for seriously ill or frail clients. It travels with the client across settings, whereas an advance directive is a legal statement of wishes that is not itself a medical order.

5.A client's family demands full resuscitation, but the client's valid advance directive refuses it. What does the nurse do?

The nurse follows the competent client's documented wishes, because the advance directive legally reflects the client's autonomy. The nurse advocates for the client, notifies the provider, and may request an ethics consult or chaplain to support the grieving family.

6.Who makes health care decisions when an incapacitated client has no advance directive or proxy?

Most states follow a surrogate hierarchy, typically a court-appointed guardian, then spouse or domestic partner, adult children, parents, adult siblings, and other relatives. The surrogate should use substituted judgment, deciding as the client would have wanted rather than by personal preference.

7.What is the nurse's role regarding a client's organ and tissue donation wishes?

Federal rules require hospitals to notify their organ procurement organization of every death or imminent death, and the trained requester from that organization usually approaches the family. The nurse supports the family, maintains the potential donor's physiologic stability, and checks registry or driver's license donor designation.

8.What is the nurse's priority action when a client says they do not understand a planned surgery?

The nurse does not let the client sign the consent and notifies the surgeon that the client needs more explanation. Explaining the procedure, risks, and alternatives is the provider's duty, and acting as the client's advocate means halting the process until understanding is achieved.

9.How does the nurse advocate for a client who disagrees with the treatment plan?

The nurse explores the client's concerns, ensures they have accurate information, communicates their wishes to the provider and team, and supports their right to refuse or choose alternatives. Advocacy means protecting the client's autonomy, not persuading them to accept what the nurse prefers.

10.What should the nurse do when receiving a provider prescription that seems unsafe or incorrect?

The nurse holds the prescription and contacts the prescriber to clarify it before acting. If the prescriber insists and the order remains unsafe, the nurse follows the chain of command, notifying the charge nurse or nursing supervisor, and documents the actions objectively.

11.What is the correct order for using the chain of command in a patient-safety concern?

The nurse first addresses the issue with the person directly involved, then goes to the charge nurse, then the nurse manager or house supervisor, and then the director of nursing or chief medical officer. Skipping levels is appropriate only if a client faces immediate danger.

12.When must the nurse use a professional medical interpreter rather than a family member?

Federal civil rights law (Title VI and Section 1557) requires qualified interpreters for clients with limited English proficiency for all health discussions, especially consent, teaching, and discharge. Family members, particularly children, should not interpret because of errors, privacy concerns, and possible filtering of information.

13.What are the Five Rights of Delegation according to the NCSBN?

The nurse delegates the right task, under the right circumstances, to the right person, with the right directions and communication, and with the right supervision and evaluation. The registered nurse remains accountable for the outcome of any task delegated.

14.Which nursing tasks can an RN never delegate to unlicensed assistive personnel?

Assessment, nursing diagnosis, care planning, client teaching, evaluation, and nursing judgment cannot be delegated to unlicensed assistive personnel. Tasks involving unstable or unpredictable clients also remain with licensed nurses.

15.What kinds of client care can a nursing assistant safely perform under RN supervision?

Routine, standardized tasks for stable clients with predictable outcomes are appropriate, such as bathing, ambulation, feeding without swallowing risk, measuring intake and output, and vital signs on stable clients. The RN must still interpret the findings that the assistant collects and reports.

16.What can a licensed practical nurse do that unlicensed assistive personnel cannot?

An LPN, or LVN, can give most oral, subcutaneous, and intramuscular medications, perform sterile procedures such as catheter insertion, wound care, and tracheostomy suctioning, and reinforce teaching for stable clients. The LPN contributes data collection but cannot perform initial assessments or create the care plan.

17.What is the difference between assignment and delegation in nursing?

Assignment transfers both responsibility and accountability for routine care that falls within the assignee's licensed scope and job description. Delegation transfers the authority to perform a specific task outside the person's usual role while the delegating nurse retains accountability for the outcome.

18.What should the RN do when an unlicensed assistant reports a client's blood pressure is 82/50?

The RN personally goes to assess the client right away, because an abnormal finding requires nursing assessment and judgment. The nurse checks for causes such as bleeding or medication effect, rechecks the reading, and notifies the provider of the change in condition.

19.What should a nurse do when floated to an unfamiliar specialty unit?

The floating nurse should accept clients whose care matches their competencies, ask for an orientation to the unit, and tell the charge nurse about tasks they are not trained to perform. Refusing the float entirely may be abandonment, but accepting unsafe specialized tasks without training is also inappropriate.

20.When is it acceptable for a nurse to refuse an assignment?

A nurse may decline an assignment that they lack the education, competence, or resources to complete safely, after explaining concerns to the charge nurse and offering alternatives. The nurse documents the concern using the facility's assignment objection process, but cannot leave once care has been accepted.

21.What constitutes client abandonment by a nurse?

Abandonment occurs when a nurse accepts a client assignment, establishing a nurse-client relationship, and then leaves without giving report or transferring care to another qualified nurse. Declining an assignment before accepting it, or leaving after proper handoff, is not abandonment.

22.How should the RN respond when an unlicensed assistant has not completed a delegated task correctly?

The RN speaks privately with the assistant, clarifies expectations, provides directions or demonstration, and verifies the task is done correctly. If performance does not improve, the RN informs the nurse manager, since the delegating RN remains responsible for supervision and evaluation.

23.What prioritization framework is most commonly used on NCLEX questions?

Use airway, breathing, and circulation first, followed by Maslow's hierarchy, with physiological needs before safety, then love and belonging, esteem, and self-actualization. Acute problems take precedence over chronic ones and actual problems over potential ones.

24.Which client should the nurse see first after receiving shift report?

The nurse sees first the client with an acute, unexpected, or life-threatening change, such as new shortness of breath, sudden confusion, or signs of shock. Expected findings related to a known diagnosis, like chronic stable edema in heart failure, can wait.

25.How does the nurse apply the concept of expected versus unexpected findings when prioritizing?

An expected finding is consistent with the client's diagnosis or recent procedure, such as mild incisional pain after surgery. An unexpected finding, such as sudden calf pain with swelling after surgery suggesting thrombosis, signals a complication and becomes the priority.

26.When does circulation take priority over airway in emergency care?

In a pulseless adult, the 2020 AHA guidelines use a C-A-B sequence, starting chest compressions within 10 seconds before opening the airway and giving breaths. Severe external hemorrhage also takes immediate priority with direct pressure or a tourniquet in trauma care.

27.What does the nurse do first when a client falls in the hospital room?

The nurse stays with the client, calls for help, and assesses for injury, including airway, level of consciousness, head trauma, and extremity deformity, before moving the client. The nurse then notifies the provider, documents factually in the record, and completes an incident report.

28.What do the four START triage colors mean in a mass casualty event?

Red means immediate care for life-threatening but survivable injuries, yellow means delayed care for serious but stable injuries, and green means minor injuries or walking wounded. Black means deceased or expected to die even with treatment, given available resources.

29.In START triage, what respiratory rate assigns an adult to the immediate red category?

An adult breathing more than 30 times per minute is tagged red, or immediate. A capillary refill longer than 2 seconds, absent radial pulse, or inability to follow simple commands also assigns the red category.

30.Which clients can be discharged first to free hospital beds during a disaster surge?

Clients who are stable, ambulatory, have support at home, and need minimal ongoing care should be discharged or transferred first, such as a client recovering from uncomplicated surgery awaiting routine discharge. Unstable clients and those requiring cardiac monitoring or frequent assessment stay.

31.How does disaster triage differ from everyday emergency department triage?

Everyday triage gives the sickest client the most resources first. Disaster triage aims to do the greatest good for the greatest number, so clients with injuries unlikely to survive given limited resources may be tagged expectant and receive comfort care only.

32.What is the hospital incident command system used for during an emergency?

The Hospital Incident Command System provides a standardized chain of command with an incident commander and defined roles for operations, planning, logistics, and finance. Nurses report to their assigned supervisor and follow the emergency operations plan instead of acting independently.

33.What three elements must be present for informed consent to be valid?

The client must have decision-making capacity, give consent voluntarily without coercion, and receive adequate information about the procedure, its risks, benefits, and alternatives, including no treatment. Consent must be obtained before preoperative sedation or opioids are given.

34.What is the nurse's role when witnessing a client's signature on a surgical consent form?

The nurse witnesses that the client signed voluntarily, appears competent, and is the person signing. Explaining the procedure and obtaining informed consent is the responsibility of the provider performing it, so the nurse notifies the provider if the client has questions.

35.When can a minor legally consent to their own health care without a parent?

Emancipated minors, such as those who are married, in the military, or financially independent, can consent to their own care. Most states also allow minors to consent to treatment for sexually transmitted infections, contraception, prenatal care, and substance use services.

36.When is informed consent not required before treatment?

In a true emergency where delay would threaten life or limb and the client cannot consent and no surrogate is available, implied consent permits treatment. The provider documents the emergency, and the nurse documents the situation and care given.

37.A client signed consent for surgery but now says they changed their mind. What should the nurse do?

Consent can be withdrawn at any time, even after signing. The nurse stops preoperative preparations, notifies the surgeon, and documents the client's statement, since proceeding would constitute battery.

38.Can a client who received a preoperative opioid sign the surgical consent form?

No. Medications that alter judgment, such as opioids or benzodiazepines, invalidate consent, so the form must be signed before sedating medication is given. If the drug was already given, the nurse notifies the provider, who decides on a surrogate or delaying the procedure.

39.Can a competent adult client refuse a blood transfusion for religious reasons?

Yes. A competent adult, such as a Jehovah's Witness, may refuse blood products even if it may result in death. The nurse ensures the client is informed of the consequences, notifies the provider, documents the refusal, and explores acceptable alternatives like volume expanders or cell salvage.

40.What must the nurse do when a client wants to leave against medical advice?

The nurse notifies the provider, explains the risks of leaving, and asks the client to sign the against medical advice form. A competent client cannot be held against their will, and if they refuse to sign, the nurse documents the refusal and the teaching given.

41.What is the difference between a voluntary and an involuntary psychiatric admission?

A voluntary client agrees to admission and may request discharge, often requiring written notice and a short holding period. An involuntary client is admitted without consent because they are a danger to self or others or gravely disabled, and keeps the right to legal counsel and a hearing.

42.Which rights does a client keep after an involuntary psychiatric admission?

Involuntarily committed clients keep the right to refuse treatment except in emergencies, to communicate privately with attorneys, to receive visitors, to send and receive mail, and to be treated in the least restrictive environment. They are not considered legally incompetent by commitment alone.

43.What are the requirements for using physical restraints on a client?

Restraints require a provider order specifying type and duration, used only after less restrictive measures fail and never for staff convenience or as punishment. Orders for violent behavior must be renewed every 4 hours for adults, and the client is monitored and documented at regular intervals.

44.How long is a restraint order valid for violent or self-destructive behavior by age?

Under CMS rules, orders last up to 4 hours for adults 18 and older, 2 hours for ages 9 to 17, and 1 hour for children under 9. A provider or trained licensed practitioner must see the client face to face within 1 hour of initiating restraint.

45.Under what circumstances can a nurse place a client in seclusion?

Seclusion is permitted only to manage violent behavior that threatens immediate physical harm to the client or others, with a provider order and after less restrictive measures fail. The client must be continuously monitored, and seclusion ends as soon as the client meets release criteria.

46.What does HIPAA's minimum necessary standard require of the nurse?

Staff should access, use, or share only the minimum protected health information needed to perform their job. Looking up the record of a coworker, celebrity, or relative not under the nurse's care violates HIPAA even if no information is shared.

47.When may the nurse disclose protected health information without client authorization?

Disclosure is permitted for treatment, payment, and health care operations, and is required for mandated reporting such as child or elder abuse, certain communicable diseases, gunshot wounds in many states, and court orders. Otherwise, written authorization from the client is needed.

48.A caller says they are the client's daughter and asks for test results. What should the nurse do?

The nurse does not share information without verifying the caller's identity and the client's permission. Many facilities use a client privacy code or password, and the nurse may instead offer to have the client call back or ask the client who may receive information.

49.What is the nurse's responsibility regarding social media and client information?

The nurse must never post client images, names, or any details that could identify a client, even without a name and even in private groups. NCSBN guidance states such posts may result in board discipline, termination, and HIPAA penalties.

50.Which client information requires stricter confidentiality protection than standard records?

Federal rule 42 CFR Part 2 adds special protection to substance use disorder treatment records, and many states add protections for HIV status, mental health, and genetic test results. These generally require specific written consent before disclosure.

51.What four elements must be proven for a nurse to be found guilty of malpractice?

The plaintiff must prove duty, meaning a nurse-client relationship existed, breach of the standard of care, causation linking the breach to the harm, and actual damages or injury. All four are required for a negligence claim.

52.What is the difference between negligence and malpractice?

Negligence is failure to act as a reasonably prudent person would in similar circumstances and can apply to anyone. Malpractice is professional negligence, where a licensed professional fails to meet the standard of care expected of their profession, causing harm.

53.What is the difference between assault and battery in health care?

Assault is a threat or attempt to touch a client that causes fear of harm, such as threatening to restrain a client who refuses medication. Battery is actual touching without consent, such as performing a procedure the client refused.

54.What constitutes false imprisonment in nursing practice?

False imprisonment is confining or restraining a client against their will without legal authority, such as using restraints without an order or preventing a competent client from leaving. It applies even when staff believe they are acting for the client's good.

55.What is the difference between libel and slander?

Both are forms of defamation, meaning false statements that harm a person's reputation. Libel is defamation in writing, including charting or social media posts, while slander is spoken defamation.

56.What legal protection do Good Samaritan laws give nurses?

Good Samaritan laws protect a nurse who gives emergency care at the scene outside of employment, without compensation, and in good faith within their training. They do not protect gross negligence or care that exceeds the nurse's competence.

57.Which situations must nurses report by law?

Nurses are mandated reporters of suspected child abuse, elder or dependent adult abuse, and certain communicable diseases to public health. Many states also require reporting of gunshot or stab wounds, and suspicion alone, not proof, triggers the duty.

58.What should the nurse do upon suspecting a colleague is impaired at work?

The nurse reports the concern promptly to the nurse manager or supervisor so the colleague is removed from client care. Confronting the colleague alone or ignoring the behavior endangers clients, and many boards offer alternative-to-discipline programs for nurses with substance use disorders.

59.What is the purpose of an incident or occurrence report?

An incident report documents an unexpected event such as a fall or medication error for internal quality improvement and risk management. It is not placed in the medical record, and the chart should not mention that a report was filed.

60.How should the nurse document a medication error in the client's chart?

The nurse objectively charts what medication was given, the client's assessment and response, the provider notification, and interventions. The chart should not include blame, the words incident report, or statements like error occurred.

61.How should the nurse correct an error in an electronic health record?

The nurse follows the facility's correction process, which marks the original entry as amended while preserving it with a date, time, and the nurse's electronic signature. Entries are never deleted, and late entries are clearly labeled as late with the current time.

62.What are the requirements for accepting a verbal or telephone order?

Verbal orders should be limited to emergencies or when the provider cannot enter orders. The nurse writes down the full order and reads it back to the prescriber for confirmation, then documents it, and the prescriber authenticates it within the time set by facility policy and state law.

63.What does the state Nurse Practice Act define?

Each state's Nurse Practice Act defines the legal scope of practice for RNs and LPNs, licensure requirements, and grounds for discipline, and it is enforced by the state board of nursing. Nurses are legally held to their own state's act.

64.What does the Nurse Licensure Compact allow a nurse to do?

The Nurse Licensure Compact lets an RN or LPN with a multistate license from their primary state of residence practice in person or by telehealth in other compact states. The nurse must follow the Nurse Practice Act of the state where the client is located.

65.Define the ethical principles of autonomy, beneficence, nonmaleficence, and justice.

Autonomy is respecting a client's right to make their own decisions, beneficence is doing good, and nonmaleficence is avoiding harm. Justice means fair and equal distribution of care and resources regardless of the client's background.

66.Define the ethical principles of fidelity and veracity.

Fidelity means keeping promises and being faithful to commitments, such as returning with pain medication when promised. Veracity means telling the truth, so the nurse does not lie to clients about their condition or medications.

67.What does the ANA Code of Ethics say is the nurse's primary commitment?

Provision 2 of the ANA Code of Ethics states the nurse's primary commitment is to the client, whether an individual, family, group, or community. Provision 1 requires compassion and respect for the inherent dignity and worth of every person.

68.What is moral distress in nursing?

Moral distress occurs when a nurse knows the ethically correct action but feels unable to take it because of institutional constraints or power differences. Ethics consultation, debriefing, and open communication help reduce it and prevent burnout.

69.Can a nurse refuse to participate in a procedure that conflicts with personal beliefs?

Conscience protections may allow a nurse to decline participation in procedures such as abortion, provided the nurse notifies the employer in advance. The nurse must not abandon a client, and must still give emergency care until another nurse takes over.

70.What is the main goal of case management in nursing?

Case management coordinates care across settings to achieve quality outcomes while controlling costs and avoiding duplication of services. The case manager assesses needs, plans, links clients to resources, monitors progress, and advocates, especially for clients with complex or chronic conditions.

71.What is the purpose of a critical pathway, or clinical pathway?

A critical pathway is a multidisciplinary plan that outlines expected interventions and outcomes on a day-by-day timeline for a specific diagnosis, such as hip replacement. Variances from the pathway are documented and analyzed to adjust care.

72.What should the nurse include in a handoff report using SBAR?

SBAR stands for situation, background, assessment, and recommendation. The nurse states the current concern, relevant history and diagnosis, current assessment findings and vital signs, and what action or plan they recommend, which standardizes communication and reduces errors.

73.When should discharge planning begin for a hospitalized client?

Discharge planning begins at admission. The nurse assesses home environment, support systems, functional status, and learning needs early so that teaching, equipment, referrals, and follow-up appointments are arranged before the discharge date.

74.What is medication reconciliation and when is it performed?

Medication reconciliation compares the client's current medication list, including over-the-counter drugs and supplements, with new orders to find omissions, duplications, and interactions. It is performed at every transition of care, including admission, transfer between units, and discharge.

75.Which client needs a referral to a social worker before discharge?

A social worker referral is needed for clients with financial barriers, lack of insurance, unsafe housing, no caregiver, suspected abuse, or a need for placement in a skilled nursing or rehab facility. Social workers also help with advance directives and community resource linkage.

76.Which client needs a referral to physical therapy versus occupational therapy?

Physical therapy addresses mobility, gait, strength, transfers, and use of walkers or crutches. Occupational therapy focuses on activities of daily living such as dressing, bathing, and eating, along with adaptive equipment and fine motor skills.

77.What criteria make a client eligible for the Medicare hospice benefit?

Two physicians must certify a terminal illness with a life expectancy of 6 months or less if the disease follows its expected course, and the client chooses comfort care rather than curative treatment for that illness. Clients can revoke hospice and resume curative care at any time.

78.What is the difference between palliative care and hospice care?

Palliative care relieves symptoms and stress of serious illness at any stage and can be given alongside curative treatment. Hospice is a type of palliative care for clients with a prognosis of 6 months or less who have stopped curative treatment.

79.What criteria qualify a client for Medicare home health services?

The client must be homebound, meaning leaving home requires considerable effort, need intermittent skilled nursing or therapy, be under a plan of care established by a provider, and have a documented face-to-face encounter. Continuous custodial care alone does not qualify.

80.What is the nurse's role during interdisciplinary team rounds?

The nurse shares current assessment findings, client and family goals, and concerns, and advocates for the client's preferences. The nurse also clarifies the plan with team members so that care is coordinated and the client receives consistent information.

81.What is the best initial approach for resolving conflict between two staff members?

Collaboration, or a win-win approach, is usually most effective, bringing the staff members together privately to discuss the issue and agree on a shared solution. Avoiding the conflict or simply imposing a decision rarely resolves the underlying problem.

82.Compare autocratic, democratic, and laissez-faire leadership styles.

An autocratic leader makes decisions alone and is most useful in emergencies. A democratic leader involves the team in decisions and builds commitment, while a laissez-faire leader gives minimal direction, which works only with highly experienced, self-motivated staff.

83.What is a nurse's best response to a provider who is verbally abusive?

The nurse stays calm, sets a professional boundary, such as stating they will continue the discussion when it is respectful, and focuses on client safety. The nurse documents and reports the behavior through the chain of command, since disruptive behavior threatens safety.

84.What is lateral violence, and how should a nurse respond to it?

Lateral violence, also called horizontal violence or bullying, is hostile behavior between peers such as sabotage, gossip, and exclusion. The nurse addresses it directly and respectfully when safe, documents incidents, and reports to management, since it harms teamwork and client outcomes.

85.What is the purpose of a root cause analysis after a sentinel event?

A root cause analysis examines a sentinel event, such as wrong-site surgery or an unexpected death, to identify system and process failures rather than blame individuals. The Joint Commission expects an analysis and action plan within 45 business days of learning of the event.

86.What are the four steps of the PDSA cycle in quality improvement?

Plan identifies a problem and proposes a change, Do tests the change on a small scale, Study analyzes the results against predictions, and Act adopts, adjusts, or abandons the change. The cycle repeats for continuous improvement.

87.What is a just culture in patient safety?

A just culture encourages staff to report errors and near misses without fear of punishment, focusing on fixing systems. It still holds individuals accountable for reckless behavior, distinguishing human error, at-risk behavior, and reckless conduct.

88.What is a near miss, and should the nurse report it?

A near miss is an error that was caught before it reached the client, such as a wrong dose intercepted at the bedside. It should be reported through the facility's safety reporting system because it reveals system weaknesses before harm occurs.

89.What should the nurse do when the barcode scanner will not read a client's medication?

The nurse does not override the system routinely or give medication without verification. The nurse rechecks the order and client identifiers, contacts pharmacy for a correct label, and reports recurring scanning problems, since workarounds defeat a key safety barrier.

90.What does the nurse do when a client asks to see their own medical record?

Under HIPAA, clients have the right to access and receive a copy of their health records, generally within 30 days of the request. The nurse follows facility policy, often involving health information management, and may stay to explain the content and answer questions.

Safety and Infection Prevention and Control

91.When is hand hygiene with soap and water required instead of alcohol-based hand rub?

Use soap and water when hands are visibly soiled, after caring for a client with Clostridioides difficile or norovirus, and before eating. Alcohol does not kill spores, so mechanical friction and rinsing are needed to remove them.

92.What is the minimum duration of friction during handwashing with soap and water?

Rub all surfaces of the hands and fingers with soap for at least 15 to 20 seconds, keeping hands lower than elbows, then rinse, dry with a paper towel, and use the towel to turn off the faucet.

93.Which precautions are used for a client with pulmonary or laryngeal tuberculosis?

Airborne precautions require a single airborne infection isolation room with negative pressure and 6 to 12 air exchanges per hour, a fit-tested N95 respirator or higher for staff, and the door kept closed at all times.

94.Which diseases require airborne precautions on the NCLEX?

Airborne precautions are used for measles (rubeola), varicella (chickenpox), disseminated herpes zoster, and tuberculosis. Nonimmune staff should not care for clients with measles or varicella when immune staff are available.

95.Which common infections require droplet precautions?

Droplet precautions are used for influenza, pertussis, mumps, rubella, diphtheria, Neisseria meningitidis meningitis, and Mycoplasma pneumonia. Staff wear a surgical mask within 6 feet of the client, and a private room is preferred.

96.How should a client on droplet or airborne precautions be transported out of the room?

Transport only when essential, place a surgical mask on the client (not an N95), notify the receiving department in advance, and have the client follow respiratory hygiene and cough etiquette during transport.

97.Which organisms and conditions require contact precautions?

Contact precautions are used for MRSA, VRE, Clostridioides difficile, scabies, lice, RSV in children, wound drainage that cannot be contained, and acute diarrhea in incontinent clients. Staff wear a gown and gloves on entering the room.

98.What is the correct order for donning personal protective equipment?

After hand hygiene, put on the gown first, then the mask or respirator, then goggles or face shield, and gloves last so the glove cuffs extend over the gown sleeves.

99.What is the CDC-recommended order for removing personal protective equipment?

Remove gloves first because they are the most contaminated, then goggles or face shield, then the gown, and the mask or respirator last after leaving the room, performing hand hygiene immediately after removing all equipment.

100.How should equipment be managed for a client on contact precautions?

Dedicate noncritical equipment such as a stethoscope, blood pressure cuff, and thermometer to the client's room. If items must be shared, they are cleaned and disinfected before use with another client.

101.Which room-cleaning agent is required after discharge of a client with Clostridioides difficile?

An EPA-registered sporicidal disinfectant, typically a chlorine bleach solution, is required because Clostridioides difficile spores resist alcohol and many quaternary ammonium cleaners and survive on surfaces for months.

102.What are the core elements of respiratory hygiene and cough etiquette?

Clients and visitors with respiratory symptoms cover the mouth and nose when coughing, use tissues and discard them, perform hand hygiene, wear a surgical mask, and sit at least 3 feet from others in waiting areas.

103.What precautions apply to a client with neutropenia and an absolute neutrophil count under 500?

Use protective environment measures such as a private room, strict hand hygiene, no fresh flowers or potted plants, no raw or undercooked foods, and screening visitors for illness. The client wears a mask when leaving the room.

104.How can the nurse best prevent central line-associated bloodstream infections?

Use maximal sterile barrier precautions during insertion, scrub the hub for at least 15 seconds with antiseptic before each access, change transparent dressings every 7 days or when soiled, and remove the line as soon as it is unnecessary.

105.Which nursing measures help prevent catheter-associated urinary tract infections?

Insert catheters only when indicated using sterile technique, keep the drainage bag below bladder level and off the floor, maintain a closed system with unobstructed flow, perform daily perineal care, and remove the catheter as early as possible.

106.Which interventions reduce the risk of ventilator-associated pneumonia?

Elevate the head of the bed 30 to 45 degrees, perform regular oral care, assess daily for sedation interruption and extubation readiness, and drain ventilator tubing condensate away from the client.

107.How is chlorhexidine skin preparation applied for surgical or line asepsis?

Apply chlorhexidine with alcohol using a back-and-forth scrubbing motion for about 30 seconds and allow it to air dry completely without blotting, which is required for its antimicrobial effect and to prevent fire risk in surgery.

108.Which areas of a sterile field are considered unsterile?

The outer 1 inch border of a sterile field, anything below the waist or table level, and any item out of the nurse's line of sight are considered contaminated. A sterile field left unattended is also considered contaminated.

109.How should the nurse pour sterile solution onto a sterile field?

Pour a small amount to discard first to clean the bottle lip, then hold the bottle 4 to 6 inches above the container and pour slowly without splashing, because wetness wicks microorganisms through the field.

110.Which hand position is correct during a surgical scrub and rinse?

Hold the hands above the elbows during surgical scrubbing and rinsing so water flows from the cleanest area, the fingertips, toward the less clean elbows, and dry with a sterile towel.

111.When opening a sterile package, which flap does the nurse open first?

Open the flap farthest from the body first, then the side flaps, and the flap nearest the body last, which avoids reaching across the sterile field and contaminating it.

112.What is the correct action after a needlestick injury from a used needle?

Immediately wash the site with soap and water, report to the supervisor and employee health, and complete an incident report. Postexposure HIV prophylaxis should begin as soon as possible, ideally within 2 hours and no later than 72 hours.

113.How should used needles and sharps be disposed of safely?

Never recap used needles; activate the safety device and place sharps immediately in a puncture-resistant, labeled sharps container at the point of use. Containers are replaced when they are three-quarters full.

114.How should a nurse handle a small blood spill on the floor?

Wearing gloves, absorb the spill with disposable towels, then clean and disinfect the area with an EPA-registered disinfectant or a freshly prepared 1 to 10 dilution of household bleach, and discard materials as biohazard waste.

115.What safety measures apply when caring for a client with a sealed radioactive implant?

Use a private room with a radiation sign, wear a dosimeter badge, limit each staff visit to about 30 minutes daily, and keep pregnant staff and visitors and those under 18 away. Staff never care for the client while pregnant.

116.What should the nurse do if a sealed radiation implant becomes dislodged?

Never touch the source with bare hands; use long-handled forceps to place it in the lead-lined container kept in the room, then notify the radiation safety officer immediately.

117.Which precautions are used when handling hazardous antineoplastic drugs?

Wear two pairs of chemotherapy-tested gloves and a disposable impermeable gown, prepare drugs in a biological safety cabinet, use closed-system transfer devices, and discard waste in labeled yellow chemotherapy containers.

118.How long are hazardous drug precautions maintained for body fluids after chemotherapy?

Body fluids are considered hazardous for at least 48 hours after most chemotherapy doses, so staff wear double gloves and a gown when handling urine, stool, or emesis, and toilets are flushed with the lid closed.

119.What is the purpose of a safety data sheet in the workplace?

A safety data sheet, required by OSHA for every hazardous chemical, lists the chemical's hazards, safe handling and storage, required protective equipment, and first aid measures after exposure. Staff must know where they are kept.

120.What is the first action for a chemical splash to the eyes?

Immediately flush the eyes at an eyewash station with a continuous stream of clean water for at least 15 minutes while holding the eyelids open, then seek medical evaluation and report the exposure.

121.How is a client with suspected Ebola or another high-consequence infection initially managed?

Isolate the client immediately in a private room with a private bathroom, limit staff entry, and use trained staff in full protective equipment with a trained observer for donning and doffing, then notify infection control and public health authorities.

122.Which two identifiers must the nurse verify before giving medication or treatment?

The Joint Commission requires at least two client identifiers, such as full name and date of birth, compared with the medication record or order. Room number and bed location are never acceptable identifiers.

123.Which interventions are part of a fall-prevention plan for a high-risk client?

Keep the bed in the lowest position with wheels locked, keep the call light and personal items within reach, provide nonskid footwear, use a bed alarm when indicated, and set a scheduled toileting routine.

124.What does the Morse Fall Scale assess in hospitalized clients?

The Morse Fall Scale scores history of falls, secondary diagnosis, ambulatory aid, IV therapy or saline lock, gait, and mental status. A total score of 45 or higher generally indicates high fall risk requiring prevention measures.

125.Which hospital procedures require a time-out before starting?

The Universal Protocol requires a preprocedure verification, site marking by the person performing the procedure, and a time-out immediately before incision in which the entire team confirms the correct client, procedure, and site.

126.Which nursing actions prevent seizure injury in a client on seizure precautions?

Keep the bed low with padded side rails, have suction and oxygen at the bedside, and during a seizure turn the client to the side and protect the head. Nothing is ever placed in the client's mouth.

127.What is the RACE acronym used for during a fire?

RACE stands for Rescue clients in immediate danger, Activate the alarm, Contain the fire by closing doors and windows, and Extinguish the fire if small or Evacuate. Rescue always comes before activating the alarm.

128.How is a fire extinguisher operated using the PASS technique?

PASS stands for Pull the pin, Aim the nozzle at the base of the fire, Squeeze the handle, and Sweep from side to side. A class ABC extinguisher works on ordinary combustibles, flammable liquids, and electrical fires.

129.How are clients evacuated during a fire on a hospital unit?

Move clients horizontally behind fire doors first, beginning with ambulatory clients and then those who need assistance, and use stairs rather than elevators. Oxygen is turned off for clients who can tolerate it.

130.Which oxygen safety measures prevent fires in the home or hospital?

Post no-smoking signs, keep oxygen at least 10 feet from open flames and sparks, avoid petroleum-based products on the face, use cotton rather than wool or synthetic fabrics, and secure cylinders upright.

131.How does the nurse prevent electrical hazards with client care equipment?

Use only three-prong grounded plugs, inspect cords for fraying, keep liquids away from devices, never use extension cords, and verify that personal devices from home have been checked by biomedical engineering.

132.What home modifications reduce fall risk in older adults?

Remove throw rugs and clutter, install grab bars by the toilet and tub, use nonskid mats, add night-lights and good stair lighting with handrails on both sides, and keep frequently used items within easy reach.

133.What is the recommended maximum water heater temperature to prevent scald burns?

Set the home water heater at or below 120 degrees F (49 degrees C) to prevent scald injuries in young children and older adults, who have thinner skin and slower reaction times.

134.Which home measures prevent carbon monoxide poisoning?

Install carbon monoxide detectors on every level and near sleeping areas, have furnaces and gas appliances inspected yearly, and never run generators, grills, or cars in enclosed spaces. Headache, nausea, and dizziness in several household members suggest exposure.

135.How often should home smoke alarms be tested and batteries replaced?

Test smoke alarms monthly and replace batteries at least once a year unless the unit has a sealed 10-year battery. Alarms are installed on every level, inside each bedroom, and outside sleeping areas, and replaced every 10 years.

136.Which teaching prevents accidental poisoning in young children at home?

Store medications and chemicals locked and out of reach in original child-resistant containers, never call medicine candy, and post the Poison Control number, 1-800-222-1222. Inducing vomiting with syrup of ipecac is no longer recommended.

137.Which teaching about firearm safety is appropriate for families with children?

Firearms should be stored unloaded in a locked container with ammunition locked separately, and keys or combinations kept away from children. Asking about firearms in homes where children play is recommended.

138.What safe sleep guidance reduces the risk of sudden infant death?

Place infants on their back on a firm, flat surface in their own crib or bassinet, room-sharing without bed-sharing, with no pillows, bumpers, blankets, or soft toys, and avoid overheating, following AAP guidance.

139.How should a car seat be positioned for infants and toddlers?

Children ride rear-facing in the back seat as long as possible, until they reach the maximum height or weight limit of the seat, often around age 2 to 4. A rear-facing seat is never placed in front of an active airbag.

140.Which body mechanics principles protect the nurse during lifting?

Keep a wide base of support, bend at the knees and hips rather than the waist, hold the load close to the body, tighten the abdominal muscles, and pivot the feet instead of twisting the spine.

141.What is the maximum weight a nurse should lift manually without assistive devices?

NIOSH recommends that health care workers lift no more than 35 pounds of client weight manually under ideal conditions. Clients heavier than this require mechanical lifts or other safe client handling equipment.

142.How should a client with one-sided weakness use a cane correctly?

Hold the cane on the stronger side, advance the cane and the weak leg together, then step through with the strong leg. When climbing stairs, step up with the strong leg first and down with the weak leg first.

143.How should a nurse position a bed when moving a client up in bed?

Raise the bed to the caregiver's waist height, lower the head of the bed flat if tolerated, lock the wheels, use a friction-reducing sheet with at least two staff, and have the client bend the knees to help push.

144.How often must a nonviolent client in restraints be monitored and reassessed?

Monitor according to facility policy, commonly at least every 2 hours, checking circulation, skin integrity, nutrition, hydration, toileting, and range of motion, and release each restraint to reassess the continued need.

145.What are the CMS renewal limits for violent or self-destructive restraint orders in adults?

Orders for restraint or seclusion for violent behavior are time-limited to 4 hours for adults 18 and older, 2 hours for ages 9 to 17, and 1 hour for children under 9, and a face-to-face evaluation is required within 1 hour.

146.How should a wrist restraint be secured to the bed?

Tie the restraint with a quick-release knot to the movable bed frame, never the side rail, and allow two fingers to fit between the restraint and the client's skin to protect circulation.

147.Can a restraint ever be applied without a provider order?

In an emergency where a client poses imminent danger, the nurse may apply a restraint first but must obtain a provider order promptly, and PRN or as-needed restraint orders are never permitted.

148.What alternatives should be tried before applying physical restraints?

Try less restrictive measures first, such as frequent rounding, reorientation, bed and chair alarms, low beds, diversional activities, moving the client closer to the nurses' station, and involving family or a sitter.

149.Why are four full side rails raised considered a restraint?

Raising all four side rails prevents a client from getting out of bed freely, which meets the definition of a restraint, and confused clients may climb over them, increasing fall injury risk.

150.Which precautions protect a client with a known latex allergy?

Place the client in a latex-safe room, flag the allergy on the chart and armband, and use only nonlatex gloves, catheters, and equipment. Ask about allergies to banana, avocado, kiwi, and chestnut, which cross-react, and keep epinephrine available for anaphylaxis.

151.What should be documented in the chart after a client fall?

Document objective facts only, including the time, how the client was found, the assessment findings, provider notification, and interventions, without assigning blame or mentioning that an incident report was completed.

152.What security measures prevent infant abduction on maternity units?

Use electronic infant tags, require matching identification bands for the infant and parents, verify staff photo identification, and teach parents never to give the infant to anyone without proper hospital identification.

153.Which clients should be discharged first during a disaster requiring surge capacity?

Discharge clients who are stable, ambulatory, and have minimal care needs, such as those awaiting discharge or recovering from minor procedures, to free beds for disaster casualties.

154.Does a client exposed to inhalational anthrax require isolation precautions?

No. Anthrax spreads from spores, not from person to person, so standard precautions are sufficient. Contaminated clothing is removed and bagged, skin is washed with soap and water, and exposed clients receive prophylactic ciprofloxacin or doxycycline for 60 days.

155.What is the first action when a client threatens staff with a weapon?

Ensure personal and others' safety by keeping a safe distance and an exit route, do not attempt to disarm the client, and call security or activate the facility's emergency code for an armed individual.

Health Promotion and Maintenance

156.How does the nurse calculate the estimated date of birth using Naegele's rule?

Take the first day of the last menstrual period, subtract 3 months, add 7 days, and adjust the year as needed. The rule assumes a regular 28-day cycle, so early ultrasound dating is preferred when the dates disagree.

157.Where should the fundus be at 20 weeks, and how is fundal height interpreted later?

At about 20 weeks the fundus reaches the umbilicus. From roughly 18 to 32 weeks, fundal height in centimeters should equal gestational age in weeks within 2 cm, and a larger gap warrants an ultrasound for growth or fluid problems.

158.When is a pregnant client routinely screened for gestational diabetes?

Screening occurs at 24 to 28 weeks of gestation, usually with a 1-hour 50 g glucose challenge test that does not require fasting. Clients with obesity or prior gestational diabetes may also be screened early in pregnancy.

159.When should a pregnant client be screened for group B streptococcus?

A vaginal-rectal culture is collected at 36 0/7 to 37 6/7 weeks of gestation. A positive result means intrapartum IV penicillin is given during labor to prevent early-onset neonatal infection.

160.When does an Rh-negative, unsensitized pregnant client receive Rho(D) immune globulin?

It is given IM at about 28 weeks and again within 72 hours after birth if the newborn is Rh positive. It is also given after miscarriage, amniocentesis, abdominal trauma, or bleeding during pregnancy.

161.Which vaccines are recommended during pregnancy to protect the newborn?

Tdap is given in every pregnancy at 27 to 36 weeks, preferably early in that window, and inactivated influenza vaccine is given in any trimester during flu season. RSV vaccine may be given at 32 to 36 weeks during September through January.

162.What folic acid dose prevents neural tube defects in clients planning pregnancy?

Clients who could become pregnant should take 400 to 800 mcg of folic acid daily, starting at least 1 month before conception. A client with a previous pregnancy affected by a neural tube defect needs 4 mg daily.

163.What dietary teaching about fish and alcohol applies during pregnancy?

Clients should eat 8 to 12 oz a week of low-mercury fish such as salmon or shrimp and avoid shark, swordfish, king mackerel, and tilefish. No amount of alcohol is known to be safe at any point in pregnancy.

164.What is the normal fetal heart rate baseline during labor?

The normal baseline is 110 to 160 beats per minute, assessed over 10 minutes. Moderate variability of 6 to 25 beats per minute is reassuring and shows adequate fetal oxygenation.

165.Where should the uterine fundus be after birth, and how does it change?

Within 12 hours after birth the firm fundus is at or slightly above the umbilicus, then descends about 1 cm per day. It is usually no longer palpable abdominally by about 2 weeks. A boggy fundus is massaged until firm.

166.What are the normal stages of lochia and their timing?

Lochia rubra is dark red and lasts about days 1 to 3, lochia serosa is pinkish brown from about days 4 to 10, and lochia alba is yellowish white and can last up to 6 weeks. Foul odor or large clots needs reporting.

167.What criteria must be met for the lactational amenorrhea method to be reliable contraception?

The client must be exclusively or nearly exclusively breastfeeding day and night, have no return of menses, and be less than 6 months postpartum. If any criterion is not met, another contraceptive method is needed.

168.When is the Apgar score assigned, and what does it evaluate?

Apgar is scored at 1 and 5 minutes after birth using heart rate, respiratory effort, muscle tone, reflex irritability, and color, each rated 0 to 2. A total of 7 to 10 is reassuring.

169.What prophylactic medications does the newborn receive shortly after birth?

The newborn receives a single IM dose of vitamin K, 0.5 to 1 mg in the vastus lateralis, to prevent bleeding. Erythromycin 0.5% ointment is applied to both eyes to prevent gonococcal ophthalmia neonatorum.

170.When is the first hepatitis B vaccine given to a newborn?

All medically stable newborns weighing at least 2,000 g receive hepatitis B vaccine within 24 hours of birth. If the birthing parent is HBsAg positive, the infant also receives hepatitis B immune globulin within 12 hours.

171.Which newborn screenings are completed before hospital discharge?

Screenings include a heel-stick blood spot for metabolic and genetic disorders after 24 hours of age, a hearing screen, and pulse oximetry for critical congenital heart disease. Pulse oximetry is done on the right hand and a foot after 24 hours.

172.How is a reactive nonstress test interpreted during pregnancy?

A reactive nonstress test shows at least two fetal heart rate accelerations of 15 beats per minute lasting 15 seconds within 20 minutes after 32 weeks of gestation. A reactive result is reassuring and indicates an intact fetal autonomic nervous system, while a nonreactive result requires further testing such as a biophysical profile.

173.How often should a breastfed newborn feed, and how is adequate intake judged?

A newborn should breastfeed 8 to 12 times every 24 hours. By day 5 or 6, about 6 or more wet diapers daily and steady weight gain show adequate intake.

174.When do the posterior and anterior fontanels normally close?

The posterior fontanel closes by about 2 months of age, and the anterior fontanel closes between 12 and 18 months. A bulging fontanel suggests raised intracranial pressure, and a sunken one suggests dehydration.

175.When are solid foods, cow's milk, and honey introduced to an infant?

Complementary solids start around 6 months, one single-ingredient food at a time every 3 to 5 days. Whole cow's milk and honey are avoided until 12 months, since honey can cause infant botulism.

176.Which Erikson stage and play type are typical of toddlers?

Toddlers, ages 1 to 3, are in autonomy versus shame and doubt, so offering simple choices supports independence. They engage in parallel play beside other children and enjoy push-pull toys, while temper tantrums and negativism are normal.

177.What is the main developmental task of adolescence, and which assessment tool is used?

According to Erikson, adolescents face identity versus role confusion, and peer approval matters greatly. The HEADSS interview, covering home, education, activities, drugs, sexuality, and suicide or depression, is done privately with confidentiality explained.

178.Which foods are choking hazards for children younger than 4 years?

Avoid whole grapes, hot dogs, nuts, popcorn, hard candy, raw carrots, and large chunks of meat or cheese. Foods should be cut into small pieces, and children should be seated and supervised while eating.

179.What are current car seat recommendations for infants and young children?

Children ride rear-facing as long as possible, until they reach the highest weight or height the seat allows. They then use a forward-facing harness seat, then a booster until the seat belt fits, and ride in the back seat until age 13.

180.What are the 2024 USPSTF breast cancer screening recommendations?

Women at average risk aged 40 to 74 should have screening mammography every 2 years. Women at high risk, such as those with a BRCA mutation, need individualized earlier and more intensive screening.

181.What are the USPSTF cervical cancer screening intervals for ages 21 to 65?

Ages 21 to 29 need cervical cytology every 3 years. Ages 30 to 65 may have cytology every 3 years, primary high-risk HPV testing every 5 years, or cotesting every 5 years.

182.At what age does colorectal cancer screening begin, and which options exist?

Average-risk adults are screened from 45 to 75 years of age. Options include colonoscopy every 10 years, annual fecal immunochemical testing, stool DNA-FIT every 1 to 3 years, or CT colonography every 5 years.

183.Who qualifies for annual lung cancer screening with low-dose CT?

Adults aged 50 to 80 with at least a 20 pack-year smoking history who currently smoke or quit within the past 15 years qualify. Screening stops once a person has not smoked for 15 years.

184.When should women be screened for osteoporosis, and what calcium intake is advised?

All women 65 and older, and younger postmenopausal women with risk factors, should have a DXA bone density scan. Women over 50 need about 1,200 mg of calcium daily plus vitamin D and weight-bearing exercise.

185.What A1C values indicate prediabetes and diabetes, and when should adults be screened?

An A1C of 5.7 to 6.4% indicates prediabetes, and 6.5% or higher indicates diabetes. ADA advises screening all adults starting at age 35, and earlier for adults with overweight or obesity plus risk factors.

186.Which adults should be screened once for HIV and hepatitis C?

USPSTF recommends HIV screening at least once for everyone aged 15 to 65 and hepatitis C screening at least once for adults aged 18 to 79. Pregnant clients are screened for both during each pregnancy, and higher-risk clients more often.

187.How does the nurse obtain an accurate blood pressure reading?

The client sits with back supported, feet flat, and arm supported at heart level after 5 minutes of rest. The cuff bladder should encircle 80% of the arm, since a cuff that is too small gives a falsely high reading.

188.How much physical activity do adults and children need each week?

Adults need at least 150 minutes of moderate aerobic activity weekly plus muscle-strengthening on 2 or more days. Children and adolescents aged 6 to 17 need at least 60 minutes of moderate to vigorous activity daily.

189.How is a tuberculin skin test read, and what result is positive for a healthcare worker?

The site is read 48 to 72 hours after injection by measuring induration, not redness. Induration of 10 mm or more is positive for healthcare workers, 5 mm for immunosuppressed clients, and 15 mm for clients with no risk factors.

190.Which adult vaccines are recommended by age under current ACIP guidance?

Everyone 6 months and older needs annual influenza vaccine, and adults need a Td or Tdap booster every 10 years. Adults 50 and older receive 2 doses of recombinant zoster vaccine 2 to 6 months apart and one pneumococcal conjugate vaccine.

191.What is the correct order of abdominal assessment, and why?

The order is inspection, auscultation, percussion, then palpation. Auscultating before touching prevents altering bowel sounds, and normal sounds occur about 5 to 30 times per minute.

192.Where is the apical pulse auscultated in an adult?

The apical pulse is heard at the fifth intercostal space at the left midclavicular line, where S1 is loudest. It is counted for a full minute when the rhythm is irregular or before giving digoxin.

193.How is the ear canal straightened for otoscopic examination in children and adults?

For children younger than 3 years, pull the pinna down and back. For older children and adults, pull the pinna up and back to straighten the canal and see the tympanic membrane.

194.Which sensory changes are a normal part of aging?

Presbyopia makes near vision difficult and usually starts in the 40s, and presbycusis causes gradual loss of high-frequency hearing. Speaking clearly in a low-pitched voice while facing the client improves communication.

195.What is the 5 A's framework for tobacco cessation counseling?

The nurse should Ask about tobacco use at every visit, Advise quitting, Assess readiness, Assist with counseling and medications like nicotine replacement or varenicline, and Arrange follow-up. Combining counseling with medication most improves quit rates.

196.How is binge drinking defined, and which tool screens for unhealthy alcohol use?

Binge drinking is 5 or more drinks for men or 4 or more for women on one occasion, within about 2 hours. The AUDIT-C is a brief validated screen used in primary care for adults.

197.What sun protection teaching prevents skin cancer?

Use broad-spectrum sunscreen with SPF 30 or higher, reapply every 2 hours and after swimming, and avoid midday sun and tanning beds. Infants younger than 6 months should be kept out of direct sun and covered with protective clothing.

198.What ABCDE warning signs should clients look for during skin self-examination?

The signs are Asymmetry, Border irregularity, Color variation, Diameter greater than 6 mm, and Evolving size, shape, or color. Any mole with these features should be evaluated by a provider.

199.When should a child have a first dental visit and receive fluoride varnish?

Fluoride varnish should be applied from the time the first tooth erupts, and the first dental visit should occur by age 1. Children should not be put to bed with a bottle of milk or juice.

200.Which teaching helps reduce falls in community-dwelling older adults?

USPSTF recommends exercise programs such as balance, gait, and strength training for adults 65 and older at higher fall risk. Remove throw rugs, add grab bars and night lights, and review medications that cause dizziness.

Psychosocial Integrity

201.What injury pattern in a child should raise suspicion of physical abuse?

Suspect abuse when injuries do not match the history or developmental stage, such as bruises on a non-mobile infant, patterned burns, immersion burns with clear stocking or glove lines, or posterior rib fractures. The nurse documents objectively and reports the concern.

202.What is the nurse's legal duty when child abuse is suspected?

Nurses are mandated reporters, so a reasonable suspicion of child abuse must be reported to child protective services or the designated authority. Proof is not required, and reporting in good faith protects the nurse from liability.

203.How should the nurse interview a client suspected of experiencing intimate partner violence?

Screen the client privately, away from the partner, using direct, nonjudgmental questions. Assess immediate safety, help create a safety plan, and provide hotline and shelter resources, while respecting that a competent adult decides whether to leave.

204.Which phase of the cycle of violence follows the acute battering incident?

The honeymoon phase follows, in which the abuser shows remorse, apologizes, and promises change. The cycle then returns to tension building, and episodes typically grow more frequent and severe over time.

205.What findings suggest elder neglect or financial exploitation?

Warning signs include poor hygiene, dehydration, untreated pressure injuries, missed medications, unexplained loss of money or property, and a caregiver who refuses to let the older client speak alone. Suspected elder abuse is reported to adult protective services.

206.How should the nurse handle evidence after a sexual assault?

Ask the client not to bathe, change clothes, or urinate before the examination if possible, and place each clothing item in a separate paper bag. Maintain the chain of custody and refer to a sexual assault nurse examiner when available.

207.What is the least restrictive order for managing an escalating, agitated client?

Begin with verbal de-escalation and reducing stimulation, then offer as-needed oral medication, and use seclusion or restraint only as a last resort when the client poses imminent danger to self or others and less restrictive measures have failed.

208.How do positive and negative symptoms of schizophrenia differ?

Positive symptoms are added experiences such as hallucinations, delusions, and disorganized speech or behavior. Negative symptoms are losses of normal function such as flat affect, alogia, avolition, anhedonia, and social withdrawal, and they respond less well to antipsychotics and impair daily functioning more.

209.How often should the nurse monitor a client in physical restraints?

Check the restrained client at least every 15 minutes for circulation, skin integrity, and safety, and release each limb at least every 2 hours for range of motion, hydration, and toileting. Never tie restraints to side rails.

210.What is a token economy in behavior modification?

A token economy reinforces desired behaviors by giving tokens or points that the client exchanges for privileges or rewards. It uses positive reinforcement and works best when expectations and rewards are clear and applied consistently by all staff.

211.How should the nurse set limits with a manipulative client?

State the limit and its consequence clearly and calmly once, then enforce it consistently across all staff and shifts. Avoid arguing, bargaining, or giving special favors, and focus on the behavior rather than the person.

212.What features define a therapeutic milieu on a psychiatric unit?

A therapeutic milieu is a safe, structured environment with clear rules, a predictable routine, and active client participation in unit activities and community meetings. Staff model healthy interaction, and the environment itself is used as a treatment tool.

213.When do alcohol withdrawal symptoms typically begin after the last drink?

Early alcohol withdrawal signs such as tremors, anxiety, tachycardia, and diaphoresis begin within 6 to 24 hours after the last drink. Withdrawal seizures may occur within 12 to 48 hours, and delirium tremens usually appears at 48 to 96 hours.

214.Which tool guides symptom-triggered benzodiazepine dosing during alcohol withdrawal?

The CIWA-Ar scale scores signs such as tremor, sweating, agitation, and hallucinations. Benzodiazepines such as lorazepam or chlordiazepoxide are given when scores reach the threshold set by protocol, commonly above 8 to 10.

215.Why is thiamine given before glucose to a client with chronic alcohol use?

Chronic alcohol use causes thiamine deficiency, and giving glucose first can precipitate or worsen Wernicke encephalopathy, which presents with confusion, ataxia, and nystagmus. Untreated, it may progress to Korsakoff syndrome with permanent memory loss and confabulation.

216.What findings indicate opioid withdrawal in a client?

Opioid withdrawal causes dilated pupils, yawning, rhinorrhea, lacrimation, piloerection, muscle aches, abdominal cramping, diarrhea, and anxiety. It is very uncomfortable but rarely life-threatening in healthy adults, unlike alcohol or benzodiazepine withdrawal.

217.What defense mechanism is most common in clients with substance use disorder?

Denial is the most common defense mechanism, in which the client minimizes or refuses to acknowledge the extent of use and its consequences. The nurse gently confronts the denial with specific facts about how the substance has affected the client's life.

218.What is the nurse's priority when a colleague appears impaired at work?

Client safety comes first, so the nurse reports the suspected impairment promptly to the charge nurse or supervisor following facility policy. Many state boards offer alternative-to-discipline programs that support treatment and monitored return to practice.

219.How long does a typical psychological crisis last?

A crisis is usually self-limiting and resolves within about 4 to 6 weeks, leading to a return to the previous level of functioning, improved functioning, or a lower level of functioning. Crisis intervention focuses on the immediate problem and current coping.

220.What is the nurse's first priority during crisis intervention?

The first priority is the client's safety, including assessing for risk of harm to self or others. The nurse then takes a directive, here-and-now approach to identify the precipitating event, the client's perception of it, and available supports and coping skills.

221.What should the nurse ask first when assessing suicide risk?

Ask directly whether the client is thinking about killing themselves, because asking does not plant the idea. If the answer is yes, assess for a specific plan, the lethality and availability of the means, intent, and past attempts.

222.Why is suicide risk higher as depression begins to lift?

As antidepressant treatment takes effect, energy often returns before mood improves, giving the client the energy to act on suicidal thoughts. A sudden calm or giving away of possessions may signal a decision to attempt suicide and requires close observation.

223.Which level of anxiety narrows perception yet allows the client to follow directions?

Moderate anxiety narrows the perceptual field, but the client can still focus and solve problems with direction. In severe anxiety the client cannot learn or follow complex instructions, and in panic the client may lose touch with reality.

224.How should the nurse care for a client experiencing a panic attack?

Stay with the client, remain calm, use short simple sentences, and move the client to a quiet, low-stimulation area. Guide slow breathing, and save teaching or exploration of causes until the anxiety has decreased.

225.Which nonpharmacologic techniques help clients manage stress?

Effective stress-management techniques include diaphragmatic breathing, progressive muscle relaxation, guided imagery, mindfulness, regular physical activity, and adequate sleep. The nurse teaches these skills when the client is calm enough to learn and encourages daily practice.

226.Which defense mechanism involves attributing one's own unacceptable feelings to another person?

Projection is attributing one's own unacceptable thoughts or feelings to someone else, such as a client who dislikes a peer stating the peer hates them. It differs from displacement, which redirects emotion from its source to a safer target.

227.What is the priority nursing concern for a client in acute mania?

Safety and physical needs are priorities because the manic client may become exhausted, dehydrated, or injured. Provide a low-stimulation setting, high-calorie finger foods and fluids that can be eaten on the move, and simple physical activities.

228.What nutritional goal applies to a client with anorexia nervosa during refeeding?

Weight restoration is gradual, often about 0.5 to 1 kg per week, and the nurse monitors for refeeding syndrome with falling phosphorus, potassium, and magnesium. The nurse supervises meals and observes the client for 1 to 2 hours afterward.

229.What finding helps distinguish delirium from dementia?

Delirium has an acute onset over hours to days, a fluctuating course, and impaired attention, and it is usually reversible once the cause is treated. Dementia develops gradually over months to years and is progressive and largely irreversible.

230.What approach helps when a client with obsessive-compulsive disorder performs rituals?

Do not interrupt or forbid the rituals initially, because doing so increases anxiety. Allow time for them, build structure into the schedule, and gradually limit the time spent on rituals as the client learns alternative coping.

231.How should the nurse care for a client with posttraumatic stress disorder?

Use a trauma-informed approach that emphasizes safety, trust, choice, and predictability, and avoid forcing the client to recount the trauma. Teach grounding techniques for flashbacks, and support evidence-based therapies such as cognitive processing or prolonged exposure therapy.

232.How should the nurse respond to a client experiencing auditory hallucinations?

Acknowledge the client's experience without agreeing, such as saying you do not hear the voices but understand they seem real. Ask whether the voices are telling the client to harm self or others, because command hallucinations are a safety priority.

233.How should the nurse respond to a client's fixed delusion?

Do not argue with or reinforce the delusion, and avoid trying to logically disprove it. Acknowledge the feelings behind it, present reality briefly, and redirect the client toward reality-based topics and activities.

234.Which communication strategies support a client with progressive dementia?

Approach from the front, speak slowly in short, simple sentences, ask one question at a time, and offer limited choices. Maintain a consistent routine and caregiver, and use validation and redirection rather than repeatedly correcting confusion.

235.Which therapeutic communication technique restates the client's main idea to confirm understanding?

Paraphrasing or restating repeats the client's main message in similar words so the client feels heard and can confirm or correct it. Clarifying asks the client to explain a vague statement further.

236.Why is asking a client why they did something nontherapeutic?

Questions that begin with why tend to sound judgmental and put the client on the defensive, making them justify behavior. Open-ended questions such as asking the client to describe what happened encourage fuller, more honest responses.

237.Which communication responses block therapeutic interaction and should be avoided?

Blocking responses include giving false reassurance, offering personal advice, changing the subject, belittling feelings, and agreeing or disagreeing with the client. These shift the focus away from the client and discourage expression of feelings.

238.During which phase of the nurse-client relationship are goals and the contract established?

In Peplau's model, the orientation phase establishes trust, the contract, confidentiality limits, and the planned termination date. The working phase addresses problems and goals, and the termination phase reviews progress and processes feelings about ending.

239.How should the nurse ensure accurate communication with a client with limited English proficiency?

Use a qualified medical interpreter, either in person, by phone, or by video, rather than family members, especially children. Speak directly to the client, use short sentences, and confirm understanding with the teach-back method.

240.How should the nurse respond when a client's religion conflicts with prescribed treatment?

Respect the client's right to make an informed decision, explore the beliefs involved, and notify the provider about acceptable alternatives. For example, many Jehovah's Witnesses refuse blood transfusions but may accept volume expanders or cell salvage.

241.How should the nurse address a client's spiritual distress?

Assess what gives the client meaning, hope, and strength, listen actively without imposing personal beliefs, and offer a referral to a chaplain or the client's own spiritual leader. Facilitate rituals, prayer, or religious practices that the client requests.

242.What are the five stages of grief described by Kubler-Ross?

The stages are denial, anger, bargaining, depression, and acceptance. Clients do not necessarily move through them in order and may revisit stages, so the nurse supports the client's current stage without forcing progression.

243.What distinguishes complicated grief from normal grief?

Complicated or prolonged grief involves intense yearning, preoccupation with the deceased, and impaired functioning lasting at least 12 months after the loss in adults. It may require referral for specialized grief therapy, whereas normal grief gradually lessens.

244.What signs indicate that death is imminent in a dying client?

Signs of approaching death include mottled, cool extremities, decreased urine output, periods of apnea or Cheyne-Stokes respirations, noisy secretions, and decreased responsiveness. Hearing is thought to remain until the end, so the nurse encourages family to keep speaking to the client.

245.How does the nurse support family members coping with a client's critical illness?

Provide honest, consistent updates, include the family in care decisions as the client wishes, and identify their support systems and coping strengths. Offer resources such as social work, chaplaincy, and support groups, and assess family members for caregiver strain.

Basic Care and Comfort

246.How should a client with a cane hold it and walk with it?

The client holds the cane on the stronger side, about 4 to 6 inches to the side of the foot. The cane and the weaker leg advance together, then the stronger leg follows.

247.How is a cane sized correctly for a client?

The top of the cane should be level with the greater trochanter, or the wrist crease with the arm hanging at the side. When holding it, the elbow should be flexed about 15 to 30 degrees.

248.What is the correct sequence for walking with a standard walker?

The client lifts the walker and moves it forward about 6 to 8 inches, steps forward with the weaker leg into the walker, then brings the stronger leg forward. The client should never step all the way to the front bar.

249.How are axillary crutches fitted to prevent nerve injury?

Leave a gap of two to three finger widths, about 1 to 2 inches, between the axilla and the crutch pad, with elbows flexed about 30 degrees. Weight rests on the hands, not the axillae, to prevent brachial plexus damage.

250.Describe the three-point crutch gait and when it is used.

Both crutches and the affected leg move forward together, then the unaffected leg steps through. It is used when one leg cannot bear weight, such as after a fracture or sprain, and the client must have good upper body strength.

251.How does a client climb and descend stairs using crutches?

Going up, the client leads with the good leg, then brings up the crutches and affected leg. Going down, the crutches and affected leg go first, followed by the good leg, remembered as up with the good, down with the bad.

252.What teaching applies to a client using a hearing aid?

Clean the earmold daily with mild soap and water, keeping the receiver dry, and remove the aid for bathing, showering, and sleep. Turn it off and open the battery door when not in use to save the battery, and store it in a dry, labeled case.

253.How should dentures be cleaned and stored by the nurse?

Line the sink with a towel and partially fill it with water to prevent breakage, brush dentures with a soft brush and cool or lukewarm water, never hot. Store them in a labeled, covered cup with water or soaking solution when not worn.

254.What is the minimum acceptable hourly urine output for an adult client?

Adult urine output should be at least 30 mL per hour, or about 0.5 mL per kg per hour. Output below this for two consecutive hours suggests hypovolemia or acute kidney injury and should be reported to the provider.

255.How does the nurse teach a client to use a sitz bath?

The client sits in warm water of about 100 to 105 degrees Fahrenheit covering the perineum for 15 to 20 minutes, two to four times daily. It promotes perineal healing and comfort after hemorrhoidectomy, episiotomy, or childbirth, and the client should rise slowly because vasodilation can cause dizziness.

256.What does a post-void residual volume indicate and what is abnormal?

Post-void residual is the urine left in the bladder after voiding, usually measured with a bladder scanner. A residual under 50 mL is normal, while volumes over 100 to 150 mL suggest retention and may need intermittent catheterization.

257.How is a clean intermittent self-catheterization taught for home use?

The client washes hands, cleans the meatus, and inserts a lubricated catheter until urine flows, typically every 4 to 6 hours. Clean rather than sterile technique is acceptable at home, and volumes should stay under about 400 to 500 mL per drainage.

258.Which exercises help clients with stress urinary incontinence?

Kegel exercises strengthen the pelvic floor by tightening the muscles used to stop urine flow, holding for about 10 seconds and relaxing 10 seconds. Clients do about 10 repetitions three times daily, with improvement usually seen after several weeks.

259.What is bladder training for urge incontinence?

Bladder training has the client void on a fixed schedule, such as every 1 to 2 hours, and gradually lengthen the interval while using urge suppression techniques. The goal is voiding every 3 to 4 hours.

260.What positioning and steps are used to administer a cleansing enema to an adult?

Place the client in the left side-lying Sims position, insert the lubricated tip 3 to 4 inches, and hold the bag no more than 12 to 18 inches above the anus. If cramping occurs, lower the bag or pause the flow.

261.Which nursing measures help prevent constipation in an immobile client?

Encourage 25 to 30 grams of fiber daily and 2 to 3 liters of fluid unless restricted, increase activity as tolerated, and offer toileting after meals to use the gastrocolic reflex. Provide privacy and an upright position on a bedside commode when possible.

262.What does a healthy stoma look like after ostomy surgery?

A healthy stoma is moist and beefy red or pink, slightly raised above the skin. A pale, dusky, purple, or black stoma indicates impaired blood supply and must be reported to the provider immediately.

263.How should the nurse size and empty an ostomy pouch?

Cut the skin barrier opening about 1/8 inch, or 2 to 3 mm, larger than the stoma to protect skin without constricting it. Empty the pouch when it is one-third to one-half full to prevent leakage and seal breakage.

264.How should a clean-catch midstream urine specimen be collected?

The client cleanses the meatus from front to back, begins voiding into the toilet, then collects the middle portion of the stream in a sterile cup. The specimen should reach the lab within 1 hour or be refrigerated.

265.How often should a bedridden client at risk for pressure injury be repositioned?

Reposition immobile clients at least every 2 hours in bed and every hour when seated in a chair. Use a 30-degree lateral tilt and keep the head of bed at or below 30 degrees when possible to reduce shear.

266.What does the Braden Scale measure and what score indicates risk?

The Braden Scale scores sensory perception, moisture, activity, mobility, nutrition, and friction and shear, with totals from 6 to 23. A score of 18 or lower indicates pressure injury risk, and lower scores mean higher risk.

267.How is a stage 2 pressure injury different from stage 1 and stage 3?

Stage 1 is intact skin with nonblanchable redness, and stage 2 is partial-thickness loss with a shallow pink wound bed or intact serum-filled blister. Stage 3 is full-thickness skin loss with visible fat but no exposed bone, tendon, or muscle.

268.Which measures prevent deep vein thrombosis in an immobile client?

Encourage early ambulation, ankle pumps, and leg exercises, and apply sequential compression devices or graduated stockings as prescribed. Avoid pillows under the knees and crossing the legs, and maintain hydration to reduce blood viscosity.

269.Which complications of immobility affect the respiratory system and how are they prevented?

Immobility causes atelectasis, pooled secretions, and hypostatic pneumonia. Prevent them with turning every 2 hours, incentive spirometry 10 times per hour while awake, coughing and deep breathing, and early mobilization.

270.Why do immobile clients develop orthostatic hypotension and how is it managed?

Prolonged bed rest reduces vascular tone and blood volume, so blood pressure drops on standing. Have the client dangle at the bedside before standing and rise slowly, and orthostatic hypotension is a systolic drop of 20 mm Hg or diastolic drop of 10 mm Hg.

271.What safe technique should be used to transfer a client from bed to wheelchair?

Lock the bed and wheelchair, position the chair at a 45-degree angle on the client's stronger side, and use a gait belt. The client pivots on the strong leg while the nurse keeps a wide base of support and bends at the knees.

272.When should a mechanical lift be used instead of manual transfer?

Evidence-based safe patient handling guidelines advise against manually lifting more than about 35 pounds of a client's weight. Use a mechanical lift or other device with adequate staff for clients who cannot bear weight or cooperate.

273.How are passive range-of-motion exercises performed safely?

Support the joint above and below, move each joint slowly and smoothly through its normal range to the point of resistance but not pain, and repeat each movement about 5 times. Stop if the client reports pain or spasm.

274.What nursing care is essential for a client in a new plaster cast?

Handle a wet plaster cast with palms, not fingertips, to avoid dents, and elevate the limb above heart level for the first 24 to 48 hours. Assess circulation, sensation, and movement distal to the cast frequently and report pain unrelieved by elevation.

275.What are key nursing checks for a client in skeletal or skin traction?

Keep weights hanging freely off the floor and do not remove them without an order, and keep ropes on pulleys and the client in proper alignment. For skeletal traction, provide pin site care per protocol and watch for signs of infection.

276.Which nonpharmacological interventions reduce pain, and how do they work?

Distraction, guided imagery, music, relaxation breathing, massage, and heat or cold reduce pain perception, and massage is thought to act through the gate control theory by stimulating large sensory fibers. They supplement prescribed analgesics rather than replace them.

277.How long should heat or cold be applied, and what are the main precautions?

Apply heat or cold for about 15 to 20 minutes, then remove it to prevent rebound effects and tissue injury. Always place a cloth barrier against the skin and check the site often, especially in clients with diabetes or impaired sensation.

278.When is cold therapy preferred over heat therapy for an injury?

Cold is preferred in the first 24 to 48 hours after an acute injury because it causes vasoconstriction, reducing swelling, bleeding, and pain. Heat is used later for muscle spasm and stiffness because it dilates vessels and increases blood flow.

279.How does a TENS unit work and when is it contraindicated?

Transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation delivers mild current through skin electrodes to block pain signals and stimulate endorphins. Avoid it in clients with pacemakers or implanted defibrillators, over the carotid sinus, on broken skin, and over the abdomen in pregnancy.

280.Which comfort measures help a dying client with labored breathing or air hunger?

Raise the head of the bed, use a fan directed toward the face, provide oral care, and keep the room calm and cool. Prescribed low-dose opioids such as morphine relieve dyspnea, and oxygen is used for comfort when it helps.

281.How should the nurse verify placement of a nasogastric tube before feeding?

Initial placement must be confirmed by X-ray before first use. Afterward, check the external tube marking, aspirate gastric contents, and test pH, where a reading of 5 or less suggests gastric placement, because auscultating an air bolus is unreliable.

282.How should a client be positioned during and after enteral tube feeding?

Keep the head of the bed elevated at least 30 to 45 degrees during continuous feedings and for at least 30 to 60 minutes after an intermittent feeding. This reduces the risk of aspiration and reflux.

283.How much water should be used to flush a feeding tube around medications?

Stop the feeding and flush the tube with at least 15 to 30 mL of water before and after each medication, and with 5 to 15 mL between multiple medications. Give each medication separately and never mix medications with formula.

284.Which feeding strategies reduce aspiration risk for a client with dysphagia?

Seat the client upright at 90 degrees, use thickened liquids and textures as ordered by speech therapy, and offer small bites with a chin-tuck swallow. Keep the client upright for 30 minutes after meals and avoid straws unless approved.

285.Which client finding suggests refeeding syndrome when nutrition is restarted?

Refeeding syndrome causes sudden drops in phosphorus, potassium, and magnesium as cells take up glucose after starvation. Watch for weakness, dysrhythmias, and confusion, and restart nutrition slowly with close electrolyte monitoring.

286.What is the correct direction for perineal care on a female client?

Cleanse from front to back, from the urethra toward the anus, using a clean part of the washcloth for each stroke. This prevents transferring fecal bacteria to the urethra and lowers urinary tract infection risk.

287.What foot and nail care teaching is essential for clients with diabetes?

Inspect the feet daily using a mirror, wash them in lukewarm water tested first, dry well between the toes, and never walk barefoot. Cut nails straight across after bathing or have a podiatrist trim them, and avoid soaking the feet.

288.How should oral care be provided to an unconscious client?

Position the client side-lying with the head turned to drain fluids, use a soft toothbrush or sponge with minimal fluid, and have suction ready. Never put fingers in the mouth, and perform oral care at least every 2 to 4 hours.

289.Which sleep hygiene measures should the nurse teach a client with insomnia?

Keep a regular sleep and wake time, use the bed only for sleep, and avoid caffeine, nicotine, alcohol, and heavy meals before bedtime. If the client cannot fall asleep within about 20 minutes, getting up for a quiet activity is advised.

290.How can the nurse promote rest for hospitalized clients at night?

Cluster care to minimize interruptions, dim the lights, reduce noise, and offer earplugs or an eye mask. Providing a back rub, a warm drink without caffeine, and toileting before sleep also promotes rest.

Pharmacological and Parenteral Therapies

291.What is the therapeutic serum range for lithium in maintenance therapy?

The maintenance therapeutic lithium level is about 0.6 to 1.2 mEq/L. Levels above 1.5 mEq/L cause toxicity with coarse tremor, vomiting, diarrhea, and confusion, so the nurse holds the dose and notifies the provider.

292.Which client factors raise the risk of lithium toxicity?

Lithium toxicity is more likely with dehydration, low sodium intake, heavy sweating, vomiting, and diarrhea. NSAIDs, thiazide diuretics, and ACE inhibitors also raise lithium levels, so the client keeps a steady sodium and fluid intake of 2 to 3 liters daily.

293.What is the therapeutic serum digoxin level for heart failure?

The usual therapeutic digoxin level is 0.5 to 2 ng/mL, with 0.5 to 0.9 ng/mL preferred in heart failure. Early toxicity signs include anorexia, nausea, vomiting, and visual changes such as yellow-green halos.

294.Which electrolyte imbalance most increases the risk of digoxin toxicity?

Hypokalemia increases digoxin binding to cardiac tissue and greatly raises toxicity risk, as do hypomagnesemia and hypercalcemia. The nurse checks potassium, especially in clients also taking loop or thiazide diuretics.

295.What is the antidote for severe digoxin toxicity?

Digoxin immune fab binds free digoxin and reverses life-threatening toxicity such as severe dysrhythmias or potassium above 5 mEq/L. Serum digoxin levels become unreliable after it is given, so the nurse monitors potassium and the cardiac rhythm instead.

296.What foods and drinks interact with warfarin therapy?

Clients on warfarin keep vitamin K intake consistent rather than avoiding leafy greens entirely, because sudden changes alter the INR. Large amounts of alcohol, cranberry juice, and supplements such as ginkgo, garlic, and ginseng raise bleeding risk.

297.What INR range is the usual target for most clients taking warfarin?

The usual INR target is 2 to 3 for atrial fibrillation, deep vein thrombosis, and pulmonary embolism. Mechanical mitral valves often require 2.5 to 3.5, and an INR well above the target increases bleeding risk.

298.What is the reversal agent for warfarin-related bleeding?

Vitamin K (phytonadione) reverses warfarin, given orally for elevated INR without bleeding or slowly IV for serious bleeding. Four-factor prothrombin complex concentrate is used with IV vitamin K for life-threatening hemorrhage because it acts within minutes.

299.What is the antidote for heparin overdose, and which lab monitors unfractionated heparin?

Protamine sulfate reverses heparin, with about 1 mg neutralizing roughly 100 units of recently given heparin. Unfractionated heparin infusions are monitored by aPTT, usually kept at 1.5 to 2.5 times the control value, or by anti-Xa levels.

300.What finding suggests heparin-induced thrombocytopenia in a client receiving heparin?

A platelet drop of 50 percent or more from baseline, usually 5 to 10 days after starting heparin, suggests heparin-induced thrombocytopenia. The nurse stops all heparin, including flushes, and anticipates a nonheparin anticoagulant such as argatroban.

301.What medications reverse dabigatran and the factor Xa inhibitors apixaban and rivaroxaban?

Idarucizumab reverses dabigatran, and andexanet alfa reverses apixaban and rivaroxaban in life-threatening bleeding. These direct oral anticoagulants need no routine INR monitoring, but renal function is checked before and during therapy.

302.What life-threatening reaction can occur with ACE inhibitors such as lisinopril?

ACE inhibitors can cause angioedema with swelling of the lips, tongue, and airway, which is an emergency requiring the drug to be stopped. A dry persistent cough is a common nuisance effect, and hyperkalemia is another important adverse effect.

303.Why are ACE inhibitors and ARBs contraindicated in pregnancy?

ACE inhibitors and angiotensin receptor blockers cause fetal renal damage, oligohydramnios, and fetal death, especially in the second and third trimesters. Clients of childbearing age need effective contraception and must report pregnancy promptly so the drug can be changed.

304.Which serious muscle effect must clients taking statins report?

Statins can cause myopathy and rare rhabdomyolysis, so unexplained muscle pain, tenderness, weakness, or dark urine must be reported. Grapefruit juice raises levels of simvastatin, lovastatin, and atorvastatin, and liver enzymes are checked if symptoms of liver injury appear.

305.What food interaction is dangerous for clients taking MAO inhibitors?

Foods high in tyramine, such as aged cheese, cured meats, draft beer, soy sauce, and fermented foods, can trigger hypertensive crisis with MAO inhibitors. Severe occipital headache, stiff neck, and palpitations require immediate care.

306.What signs indicate serotonin syndrome in a client taking an SSRI?

Serotonin syndrome causes agitation, confusion, hyperthermia, tachycardia, diaphoresis, tremor, hyperreflexia, and muscle clonus. It usually follows combining serotonergic drugs such as SSRIs with MAO inhibitors, tramadol, linezolid, triptans, or St John's wort.

307.Which findings suggest neuroleptic malignant syndrome in a client on antipsychotics?

Neuroleptic malignant syndrome presents with high fever, lead-pipe muscle rigidity, altered mental status, autonomic instability, and markedly elevated creatine kinase. The nurse stops the antipsychotic, cools the client, and anticipates dantrolene or bromocriptine.

308.What are the therapeutic serum levels for phenytoin and its common adverse effects?

The total phenytoin therapeutic range is 10 to 20 mcg/mL. Gingival hyperplasia, nystagmus, ataxia, and hirsutism are common adverse effects, and good oral hygiene with regular dental visits is taught.

309.How should IV phenytoin be administered safely?

IV phenytoin is given no faster than 50 mg per minute in adults and mixed only with normal saline because it precipitates in dextrose. Faster infusion can cause hypotension and dysrhythmias, so cardiac monitoring is used.

310.What are the major adverse effects of aminoglycosides such as gentamicin?

Aminoglycosides cause nephrotoxicity and ototoxicity, including tinnitus, hearing loss, and vertigo. The nurse monitors creatinine, urine output, and trough levels and reports any hearing changes promptly.

311.When are peak and trough levels drawn for an IV antibiotic such as vancomycin?

A trough level is drawn within 30 minutes before the next dose, and a peak level is drawn about 30 to 60 minutes after an IV infusion ends. High troughs raise toxicity risk, while low peaks suggest a dose too small to be effective.

312.What is vancomycin infusion reaction, and how is it prevented?

Vancomycin infusion reaction, formerly red man syndrome, causes flushing, itching, and rash of the face, neck, and upper body when the drug is infused too quickly. It is prevented by infusing over at least 60 minutes.

313.Which antibiotic class carries a boxed warning for tendon rupture?

Fluoroquinolones such as ciprofloxacin and levofloxacin can cause tendinitis and tendon rupture, especially in older adults and clients on corticosteroids. Other warnings include peripheral neuropathy and QT prolongation, and antacids or iron should be separated from the dose.

314.What should the nurse ask before giving the first dose of a penicillin or cephalosporin?

The nurse asks about prior allergic reactions, since anaphylaxis can occur, and stays alert for wheezing, hives, or hypotension, especially within 30 minutes. Clients with a history of severe penicillin anaphylaxis need careful evaluation before receiving cephalosporins.

315.What are key adverse effects of isoniazid and rifampin in tuberculosis treatment?

Isoniazid causes hepatotoxicity and peripheral neuropathy, which pyridoxine helps prevent. Rifampin turns urine, sweat, and tears orange-red, can stain contact lenses, and reduces the effectiveness of hormonal contraceptives.

316.What long-term adverse effects occur with chronic corticosteroid therapy?

Long-term corticosteroids cause hyperglycemia, osteoporosis, infection risk, weight gain, fluid retention, peptic ulcers, and cushingoid features. Doses are tapered rather than stopped abruptly to avoid adrenal crisis.

317.What adverse effects of NSAIDs require monitoring in older adults?

NSAIDs increase the risk of gastrointestinal bleeding, kidney injury, fluid retention, and cardiovascular events such as myocardial infarction and stroke. They are used at the lowest effective dose for the shortest time, and dark tarry stools are reported.

318.What is the maximum daily acetaminophen dose for a healthy adult?

Healthy adults should take no more than 4 grams of acetaminophen in 24 hours, and many guidelines advise 3 grams or less for older adults, chronic alcohol users, and clients with liver disease. Hidden acetaminophen in combination products must be counted.

319.What is the antidote for acetaminophen overdose?

Acetylcysteine is the antidote for acetaminophen overdose and is most effective when started within 8 to 10 hours of ingestion. It replenishes glutathione to prevent liver necrosis, and the oral form has a strong rotten-egg odor.

320.How should levothyroxine be taken for best absorption?

Levothyroxine is taken on an empty stomach, 30 to 60 minutes before breakfast, with a full glass of water. Calcium, iron, and antacids should be separated by at least 4 hours, and signs of excess such as palpitations and insomnia are reported.

321.What symptoms of hypoglycemia should clients taking insulin or sulfonylureas recognize?

Hypoglycemia, a glucose below 70 mg/dL, causes shakiness, sweating, tachycardia, hunger, confusion, and irritability. The client takes 15 grams of fast-acting carbohydrate and rechecks glucose in 15 minutes.

322.When should metformin be held for diagnostic imaging?

Metformin is held at the time of or before iodinated contrast in clients with reduced kidney function and restarted after 48 hours if renal function is stable. This precaution reduces the risk of lactic acidosis.

323.What must the nurse verify before beginning a blood transfusion?

Two qualified staff verify the provider order, signed consent, client identity with two identifiers, ABO and Rh compatibility, unit number, and expiration at the bedside. Baseline vital signs are recorded immediately before starting the transfusion.

324.Which IV fluid and catheter size are used to transfuse packed red blood cells?

Packed red blood cells are given with normal saline only, through a blood administration set with a filter. An 18 to 20 gauge catheter is preferred for adults, and dextrose or lactated Ringer's solutions must never be used.

325.How long can a unit of packed red blood cells take to infuse?

Each unit of packed red blood cells must be completely infused within 4 hours of leaving the blood bank to limit bacterial growth. The infusion should begin within about 30 minutes of pickup.

326.How should the nurse monitor a client during the first 15 minutes of a transfusion?

The nurse stays with the client and infuses slowly, about 2 mL per minute, during the first 15 minutes because most severe reactions occur early. Vital signs are rechecked after 15 minutes, then per facility policy.

327.What are the first nursing actions for a suspected transfusion reaction?

The nurse stops the transfusion immediately, keeps the IV line open with normal saline through new tubing, and stays with the client. The provider and blood bank are notified, and the blood bag and tubing are returned for testing.

328.What findings indicate an acute hemolytic transfusion reaction?

Acute hemolytic reaction from ABO incompatibility causes fever, chills, low back or flank pain, hypotension, tachycardia, and dark red urine. It can progress to kidney failure and disseminated intravascular coagulation.

329.How does transfusion-associated circulatory overload differ from transfusion-related acute lung injury?

Circulatory overload causes dyspnea, crackles, hypertension, and jugular venous distension and responds to diuretics. Transfusion-related acute lung injury causes sudden hypoxemia, fever, and hypotension with noncardiac pulmonary edema, usually within 6 hours.

330.Which blood type is the universal red cell donor and which is the universal plasma donor?

Type O negative red blood cells can be given to clients of any ABO type in an emergency. Type AB plasma is the universal plasma donor because it lacks anti-A and anti-B antibodies.

331.How much should one unit of packed red cells raise hemoglobin?

One unit of packed red blood cells raises hemoglobin by about 1 g/dL and hematocrit by about 3 percent in an average adult. Most stable hospitalized adults are transfused when hemoglobin falls below 7 g/dL.

332.How are platelets and fresh frozen plasma administered?

Platelets are infused quickly, usually over 15 to 30 minutes, and are never refrigerated. Fresh frozen plasma replaces clotting factors, must be ABO compatible, and is given promptly once thawed.

333.What is the priority action if an air embolism is suspected with a central line?

The nurse clamps the catheter, places the client in the left lateral Trendelenburg position, gives oxygen, and notifies the provider. Sudden dyspnea, chest pain, hypotension, and a churning heart murmur suggest air embolism.

334.How should a client be positioned during central venous catheter removal?

The client lies flat or in Trendelenburg and performs the Valsalva maneuver or exhales as the catheter is withdrawn. An occlusive dressing is applied to the site to prevent air embolism.

335.What confirms correct placement of a newly inserted central venous catheter before use?

A chest x-ray or other approved tip confirmation must verify placement at the lower superior vena cava near the cavoatrial junction before use. The x-ray also helps rule out pneumothorax after subclavian or jugular insertion.

336.How often is a transparent central line dressing changed?

A transparent semipermeable central line dressing is changed every 7 days, and a gauze dressing every 2 days. Any dressing that is damp, loose, or visibly soiled is changed immediately using sterile technique.

337.What bundle measures help prevent central line-associated bloodstream infections?

Prevention includes hand hygiene, chlorhexidine skin antisepsis, scrubbing the hub for at least 5 to 15 seconds before each access, and sterile dressing changes. The line is removed promptly when no longer needed.

338.Why should only a 10 mL or larger syringe be used to flush a PICC line?

Smaller syringes generate higher pressure and can rupture the catheter. A 10 mL syringe or larger is used with a pulsatile push-pause technique, and blood pressure is never measured on the arm with the PICC.

339.How is an implanted port accessed for infusion?

An implanted port is accessed with a sterile noncoring Huber needle after cleaning the skin with chlorhexidine. Blood return is checked before use, and the port is flushed per policy, often monthly, when not in use.

340.How is a weight-based dose calculated for a client weighing 154 pounds?

Divide pounds by 2.2 to convert to kilograms, so 154 pounds equals 70 kg. A 5 mg/kg order would then require 350 mg.

341.What is the formula for calculating an IV drip rate in drops per minute?

Drops per minute equal the total volume in mL multiplied by the drop factor, divided by the time in minutes. For example, 1,000 mL over 8 hours with a 15 gtt/mL set runs at about 31 drops per minute.

342.How is an infusion pump rate calculated for 1,000 mL over 8 hours?

Pump rate in mL per hour equals total volume divided by total hours. A 1,000 mL bag over 8 hours runs at 125 mL per hour.

343.How is the rate for a heparin infusion of 25,000 units in 250 mL calculated?

The concentration is 100 units per mL, so dividing the ordered units per hour by 100 gives mL per hour. An order for 1,200 units per hour runs at 12 mL per hour.

344.Which abbreviations should never be used in medication orders?

The Joint Commission prohibits U for units, IU, QD, QOD, trailing zeros such as 1.0 mg, and missing leading zeros such as .5 mg. Write units, daily, every other day, and 0.5 mg instead.

345.What outcome shows that furosemide therapy is effective?

Effective furosemide therapy produces increased urine output, daily weight loss, reduced edema, and clearer lung sounds. The nurse monitors potassium for hypokalemia and watches for ototoxicity with rapid IV doses.

346.Which diuretic class conserves potassium, and what electrolyte risk does it carry?

Potassium-sparing diuretics such as spironolactone can cause hyperkalemia, especially with ACE inhibitors, ARBs, or kidney disease. Clients avoid potassium supplements and salt substitutes, and spironolactone can cause gynecomastia.

347.What should the nurse assess before giving a beta blocker such as metoprolol?

The nurse checks the apical pulse and blood pressure and holds the dose and notifies the provider if the heart rate is below 60 or the client is hypotensive per parameters. Beta blockers should not be stopped abruptly because rebound tachycardia and angina can occur.

348.How is sublingual nitroglycerin used for acute angina?

The client sits, takes one tablet under the tongue every 5 minutes up to 3 doses, and calls 911 if pain persists 5 minutes after the first dose. Nitroglycerin is contraindicated within 24 to 48 hours of phosphodiesterase-5 inhibitors such as sildenafil.

349.What onset and peak characterize rapid-acting insulin lispro?

Insulin lispro begins working in about 15 minutes, peaks at 1 to 2 hours, and lasts 3 to 5 hours. It is given within 15 minutes before a meal, so food must be available.

350.What is unique about insulin glargine compared with other insulins?

Insulin glargine is a long-acting basal insulin with no pronounced peak that lasts about 24 hours. It is given once daily at the same time and must never be mixed with other insulins in the same syringe.

351.When mixing regular and NPH insulin, which is drawn up first?

Regular insulin, which is clear, is drawn into the syringe before cloudy NPH insulin. Air is injected into the NPH vial first, then the regular vial, to avoid contaminating the regular insulin with NPH.

352.Which site is preferred for intramuscular injections in adults?

The ventrogluteal site is preferred for adult intramuscular injections because it is free of major nerves and blood vessels. The deltoid is used for small volumes up to 1 mL, such as vaccines, and the vastus lateralis is preferred in infants.

353.What technique is used to give iron dextran intramuscularly?

The Z-track technique is used for iron and other irritating intramuscular drugs to seal the medication in the muscle. The skin is pulled laterally, the drug injected, the needle held 10 seconds, then the skin released without massage.

354.How should enoxaparin be administered subcutaneously?

Enoxaparin is injected into the abdomen at least 2 inches from the umbilicus without expelling the prefilled syringe air bubble. The nurse does not aspirate or rub the site to reduce bruising.

355.How are medications given through a nasogastric or gastrostomy tube?

The nurse verifies tube placement, flushes with 15 to 30 mL of water before and after each drug, and gives each drug separately. Enteric-coated and extended-release tablets must never be crushed.

356.Which medications should not be crushed before administration?

Extended-release, sustained-release, enteric-coated, and sublingual tablets should not be crushed, because crushing can release a dangerous dose at once or destroy the coating. The nurse asks the pharmacy for a liquid or alternate form.

357.How does the nurse give eye drops correctly?

The client tilts the head back while the nurse places drops into the lower conjunctival sac, then presses the inner canthus for 1 to 2 minutes to limit systemic absorption. Wait at least 5 minutes between different eye medications.

358.How should a client use a metered-dose inhaler with a corticosteroid and a bronchodilator?

The client uses the bronchodilator first, waits about 5 minutes, then uses the corticosteroid inhaler. Rinsing the mouth after the corticosteroid prevents oral candidiasis.

359.How should a transdermal fentanyl patch be applied and removed?

The patch is applied to clean, dry, hairless skin and replaced every 72 hours, with the old patch removed and folded sticky sides together. Heat sources such as heating pads increase absorption and can cause overdose.

360.Which medication strategies are used for extravasation of a vesicant drug?

The nurse stops the infusion, leaves the catheter in place to aspirate residual drug, and notifies the provider. Specific antidotes may be given, such as phentolamine for norepinephrine or dexrazoxane for anthracyclines.

361.What are the rights of medication administration a nurse checks?

The core rights are right client, drug, dose, route, and time, plus right documentation, reason, response, and the client's right to refuse. Two identifiers such as name and birth date are used to confirm the client.

362.What is the antidote for opioid overdose, and how is it monitored?

Naloxone reverses opioid-induced respiratory depression and is repeated every 2 to 3 minutes as needed. Its effect may end before the opioid does, so respiratory rate and sedation are monitored closely afterward.

363.Which assessment is the priority before giving an opioid?

Sedation level and respiratory rate are assessed before every opioid dose, since excessive sedation precedes respiratory depression. A respiratory rate below 12 or difficulty rousing the client requires holding the dose and notifying the provider.

364.What safety rule applies to patient-controlled analgesia?

Only the client should press the patient-controlled analgesia button, because family pushing it can cause overdose. The nurse monitors sedation, respiratory rate, and pulse oximetry or capnography.

365.What adverse effect of opioids should be prevented throughout therapy?

Constipation persists with opioid use and does not improve with tolerance, so a stimulant laxative with a stool softener is started with therapy. Fluid intake, fiber, and activity are encouraged as tolerated.

366.Which nursing assessment is needed for a client with epidural analgesia?

The nurse monitors respiratory rate, sedation, blood pressure, and sensory and motor blockade and watches for hypotension. Anticoagulant timing is coordinated to reduce the risk of epidural hematoma.

367.What lab value is monitored closely in a client receiving TPN?

Blood glucose is monitored every 4 to 6 hours initially because the high dextrose content can cause hyperglycemia. Electrolytes, triglycerides, liver function, and daily weight are also monitored.

368.What should the nurse do if the TPN bag runs out before the next is available?

The nurse hangs 10 percent dextrose in water at the same rate to prevent rebound hypoglycemia. TPN is never stopped abruptly, and it is tapered when discontinued.

369.How should total parenteral nutrition be administered to prevent infection?

TPN is given through a central line with a dedicated lumen and an inline filter, and tubing is changed every 24 hours. A TPN bag hangs no longer than 24 hours, and lipid emulsions alone hang no longer than 12 hours.

370.What is refeeding syndrome, and which client is at risk when TPN starts?

Refeeding syndrome causes dangerous drops in phosphorus, potassium, and magnesium when feeding begins in a severely malnourished client. Nutrition is started slowly, and electrolytes are monitored closely during the first days.

Reduction of Risk Potential

371.What is the normal serum potassium range for an adult client?

Normal serum potassium is 3.5 to 5.0 mEq/L. Levels outside this range can cause dangerous dysrhythmias, so the nurse reports abnormal values promptly and places the client on cardiac monitoring when levels are markedly high or low.

372.What is the normal adult serum sodium range, and what does hyponatremia cause?

Normal serum sodium is 135 to 145 mEq/L. Hyponatremia below 135 can cause confusion, headache, and seizures, and chronic hyponatremia must be corrected slowly to prevent osmotic demyelination syndrome.

373.Which ECG changes are characteristic of hyperkalemia?

Hyperkalemia produces tall, peaked T waves first, followed by a prolonged PR interval, widened QRS complex, and loss of P waves. Severe hyperkalemia can progress to ventricular fibrillation or asystole.

374.Which ECG finding is classic for hypokalemia?

Hypokalemia causes flattened or inverted T waves, ST depression, and a prominent U wave. The client may also report muscle weakness and leg cramps and is at higher risk of digoxin toxicity.

375.What is the therapeutic INR range for a client taking warfarin for atrial fibrillation?

For most indications, including atrial fibrillation and venous thromboembolism, the target INR is 2.0 to 3.0. Clients with certain mechanical mitral valves may have a target of 2.5 to 3.5.

376.Which platelet count places a client at risk for spontaneous bleeding?

Normal platelets are 150,000 to 400,000 per microliter. Below 50,000 bleeding risk rises with procedures, and below 20,000 spontaneous bleeding can occur, so the nurse institutes bleeding precautions and avoids injections and rectal procedures.

377.What hemoglobin A1C level confirms diabetes mellitus?

An A1C of 6.5 percent or higher confirms diabetes, and 5.7 to 6.4 percent indicates prediabetes. The A1C reflects average blood glucose over roughly the previous three months.

378.What are normal arterial blood gas values for pH, PaCO2, and bicarbonate?

Normal arterial pH is 7.35 to 7.45, PaCO2 is 35 to 45 mm Hg, and bicarbonate is 22 to 26 mEq/L. Normal PaO2 is 80 to 100 mm Hg.

379.Interpret ABG: pH 7.30, PaCO2 55 mm Hg, HCO3 25 mEq/L.

This is uncompensated respiratory acidosis because the pH is low, the PaCO2 is high, and bicarbonate is still normal. Common causes include hypoventilation from opioid overdose, COPD exacerbation, or respiratory muscle weakness.

380.Which absolute neutrophil count indicates severe neutropenia requiring protective precautions?

An absolute neutrophil count below 500 cells per microliter is severe neutropenia with high infection risk. The nurse implements neutropenic precautions, monitors temperature closely, and reports any fever of 38 degrees Celsius or higher immediately.

381.What serum creatinine and BUN values are normal in adults?

Normal serum creatinine is about 0.6 to 1.2 mg/dL and BUN is 10 to 20 mg/dL. A rising creatinine is the more specific indicator of declining kidney function and may require dose adjustment of renally cleared drugs.

382.What is the normal white blood cell count, and what does a left shift indicate?

The normal adult white blood cell count is about 5,000 to 10,000 per cubic millimeter. A left shift means an increase in immature band neutrophils, which signals an acute bacterial infection as the bone marrow releases young cells to meet demand.

383.What is the therapeutic serum digoxin level, and which electrolyte increases toxicity?

The usual therapeutic digoxin level is 0.5 to 2.0 ng/mL, with lower targets often used in heart failure. Hypokalemia increases toxicity, which presents with nausea, visual halos, and bradycardia.

384.How are vancomycin levels monitored to reduce nephrotoxicity risk?

Current guidance favors area under the curve dosing, and when trough levels are used they are drawn just before the next dose. The nurse also monitors creatinine and urine output for nephrotoxicity.

385.Which serum calcium range is normal, and what signs indicate hypocalcemia?

Normal total serum calcium is about 9.0 to 10.5 mg/dL. Hypocalcemia causes tingling around the mouth, muscle cramps, and positive Chvostek and Trousseau signs, and can lead to laryngospasm or seizures.

386.What is the normal serum magnesium range, and which reflex is lost in toxicity?

Normal serum magnesium is 1.3 to 2.1 mEq/L. Hypermagnesemia depresses deep tendon reflexes, and their loss is an early warning sign that respiratory depression and cardiac arrest may follow.

387.What does an elevated troponin level indicate in a client with chest pain?

Elevated cardiac troponin indicates myocardial injury and supports a diagnosis of myocardial infarction when paired with symptoms or ECG changes. High-sensitivity troponin is drawn on arrival and repeated within one to three hours to detect a rise.

388.What does an elevated BNP level suggest in a client with dyspnea?

B-type natriuretic peptide is released when ventricles are stretched, so a BNP above about 100 pg/mL supports heart failure as the cause of dyspnea. Levels also help track response to heart failure treatment.

389.What blood pressure reading defines stage 2 hypertension in adults?

Under AHA and ACC guidelines, stage 2 hypertension is a systolic pressure of 140 or higher or a diastolic pressure of 90 or higher. Stage 1 is 130 to 139 systolic or 80 to 89 diastolic.

390.Which vital sign pattern makes up Cushing triad in increased intracranial pressure?

Cushing triad consists of a widening pulse pressure with rising systolic pressure, bradycardia, and irregular respirations. It is a late, ominous sign of brainstem compression requiring immediate notification of the provider.

391.How does the nurse assess for orthostatic hypotension?

The nurse measures blood pressure and pulse lying down, then after standing for one to three minutes. A drop of 20 mm Hg or more systolic or 10 mm Hg diastolic indicates orthostatic hypotension and fall risk.

392.What target oxygen saturation is appropriate for a client with chronic CO2 retention from COPD?

For clients with COPD who retain carbon dioxide, the usual target SpO2 is 88 to 92 percent. Excess oxygen can worsen hypercapnia, so oxygen is titrated carefully while monitoring mental status and blood gases.

393.What does a narrowing pulse pressure suggest after chest trauma?

A narrowing pulse pressure with muffled heart sounds and jugular venous distention, known as Beck triad, suggests cardiac tamponade. Pulsus paradoxus greater than 10 mm Hg may also be present, and emergency pericardiocentesis may be needed.

394.What must the nurse verify before a client receives iodinated contrast for a CT scan?

The nurse checks for prior contrast reactions, kidney function through serum creatinine or eGFR, and metformin use. Metformin is commonly held at the time of contrast and for 48 hours after in clients with reduced kidney function or acute kidney injury.

395.Which items must be removed or screened before an MRI?

All ferromagnetic objects must be removed, and the client is screened for pacemakers, cochlear implants, aneurysm clips, insulin pumps, and medication patches containing metal. Some implanted devices are MRI conditional and require specific protocols.

396.What post-procedure care follows a cardiac catheterization with femoral access?

The client keeps the affected leg straight for several hours while the nurse checks the insertion site for bleeding or hematoma and monitors distal pulses, color, and temperature. Increased fluids help clear contrast dye.

397.What position and monitoring follow a liver biopsy?

After a liver biopsy, the client lies on the right side with a pillow under the costal margin for several hours to compress the site. The nurse monitors vital signs closely for hemorrhage, since the liver is highly vascular.

398.What client teaching applies before a colonoscopy?

The client follows a clear liquid diet the day before and completes the prescribed bowel preparation until stool is clear yellow liquid. Red and purple liquids are avoided, and a driver is needed because of sedation.

399.After a thoracentesis, which findings require immediate reporting?

The nurse reports sudden dyspnea, tachycardia, decreased breath sounds on the affected side, or falling oxygen saturation, which suggest pneumothorax. A chest x-ray is commonly obtained afterward to confirm lung reexpansion.

400.Why is the gag reflex checked after an upper endoscopy?

Topical anesthetic suppresses the gag reflex, so the client remains on nothing by mouth until the reflex returns to prevent aspiration. The nurse also watches for throat pain, fever, or bleeding suggesting perforation.

401.What position helps prevent a headache after lumbar puncture?

Many protocols keep the client flat for a short period and encourage fluids to replace cerebrospinal fluid. A severe positional headache that persists may indicate a leak and can be treated with an epidural blood patch.

402.How is a 24-hour urine collection started and completed correctly?

The client discards the first morning void and notes the time, then collects all urine for the next 24 hours, ending with a final void at the same time. The container is kept refrigerated or on ice.

403.What should a client avoid before an exercise or pharmacologic stress test?

The client avoids caffeine for about 12 to 24 hours because it blocks adenosine and regadenoson, and may need to hold beta blockers as prescribed. The client fasts for several hours and wears comfortable shoes.

404.How should a blood culture be collected when antibiotics are also prescribed?

Blood cultures are drawn from two separate sites using strict aseptic technique before the first antibiotic dose. Drawing cultures first improves organism detection, but antibiotics for suspected sepsis should not be significantly delayed.

405.Which clients need aspiration precautions, and what are the key interventions?

Clients with dysphagia, decreased consciousness, or stroke need a swallow screen before oral intake. Key interventions include sitting upright at 90 degrees for meals, using thickened liquids if ordered, and keeping the head of bed elevated afterward.

406.How does the nurse use the Braden Scale to reduce pressure injury risk?

The Braden Scale scores sensory perception, moisture, activity, mobility, nutrition, and friction and shear, with a total of 18 or lower indicating risk. Lower scores call for repositioning schedules, support surfaces, and nutritional support.

407.Which signs indicate compartment syndrome after a fracture or cast?

Early compartment syndrome causes pain out of proportion to the injury and pain with passive stretch, followed by paresthesia, pallor, and pulselessness. The nurse elevates the limb to heart level, loosens dressings, and notifies the provider immediately.

408.What finding suggests a deep vein thrombosis in an immobile client?

Unilateral calf or thigh swelling, warmth, redness, and tenderness suggest DVT, and the diagnosis is confirmed with venous duplex ultrasound. The nurse does not massage the limb, because a clot could dislodge and cause pulmonary embolism.

409.Which assessment findings suggest a pulmonary embolism?

Sudden dyspnea, pleuritic chest pain, tachycardia, tachypnea, anxiety, and low oxygen saturation suggest pulmonary embolism. The nurse raises the head of the bed, applies oxygen, and notifies the rapid response team or provider.

410.What are early signs of sepsis that the nurse should recognize?

Early sepsis signs include fever or hypothermia, tachycardia, tachypnea, new confusion, and hypotension, with elevated serum lactate. A lactate of 2 mmol/L or more warrants urgent evaluation and initiation of the sepsis bundle.

411.Which findings indicate fluid volume overload?

Fluid overload presents with bounding pulses, jugular venous distention, crackles in the lungs, edema, rapid weight gain, and elevated blood pressure. A weight gain of 1 kg reflects about 1 liter of retained fluid.

412.Which components make up the Glasgow Coma Scale, and what score suggests coma?

The Glasgow Coma Scale scores eye opening, verbal response, and motor response, with a range of 3 to 15. A score of 8 or less indicates severe brain injury and usually the need to protect the airway.

413.What is the earliest sign of increasing intracranial pressure?

A change in level of consciousness, such as restlessness, confusion, or new drowsiness, is the earliest and most sensitive sign of rising intracranial pressure. Pupil changes and vital sign changes occur later.

414.How is a neurovascular check performed on an extremity?

The nurse compares the affected and unaffected limbs for color, temperature, capillary refill, pulses, sensation, movement, and pain. Capillary refill longer than 3 seconds or new numbness requires prompt reporting.

415.Which bowel sound finding after abdominal surgery may indicate paralytic ileus?

Absent or markedly hypoactive bowel sounds with abdominal distention, nausea, and no passage of flatus suggest paralytic ileus. The client is kept nothing by mouth and may need nasogastric decompression.

416.What does a low serum albumin level indicate in a hospitalized client?

Normal serum albumin is 3.5 to 5.0 g/dL. A low level reflects protein malnutrition, liver disease, or protein loss and lowers oncotic pressure, causing edema and delayed wound healing, and it increases the free fraction of highly protein-bound drugs such as phenytoin and warfarin.

417.What should the nurse do if wound evisceration occurs after abdominal surgery?

If wound dehiscence with evisceration occurs, the nurse covers the organs with sterile saline-moistened dressings, places the client in low Fowler position with knees bent, and notifies the surgeon. The client stays nothing by mouth.

418.What signs suggest malignant hyperthermia during or after anesthesia?

Malignant hyperthermia causes a rising end-tidal carbon dioxide, muscle rigidity, tachycardia, and a late high fever after succinylcholine or volatile anesthetics. Treatment is immediate dantrolene, stopping the triggering agent, and active cooling.

419.Which postoperative finding suggests hemorrhage and hypovolemic shock?

Tachycardia, falling blood pressure, cool clammy skin, restlessness, and decreased urine output below 0.5 mL/kg per hour suggest hemorrhage. The nurse checks the dressing and underneath the client for pooled blood and notifies the surgeon.

420.When should a client after thyroidectomy be assessed for hypocalcemia?

Accidental injury to the parathyroid glands can cause hypocalcemia within 24 to 48 hours. The nurse checks for tingling and muscle twitching and keeps IV calcium gluconate and a tracheostomy set available.

421.Which finding after total hip arthroplasty suggests dislocation?

Sudden pain, a shortened limb, and internal or external rotation of the leg suggest hip dislocation. Clients with a posterior approach avoid hip flexion beyond 90 degrees, crossing the legs, and internal rotation.

422.How is incentive spirometry used to prevent postoperative atelectasis?

The client sits upright, exhales, seals the lips around the mouthpiece, and inhales slowly and deeply, holding the breath for 3 to 5 seconds. This is repeated about 10 times every hour while awake.

423.What are signs of a transfusion reaction, and what is the first nursing action?

Fever, chills, back pain, hives, wheezing, or hypotension during a transfusion suggest a reaction. The nurse stops the transfusion immediately, maintains the IV with normal saline using new tubing, and notifies the provider and blood bank.

424.How does the nurse confirm nasogastric tube placement before use?

Initial placement of a blind-inserted feeding tube is confirmed by x-ray before the first feeding. Afterward the nurse checks external tube markings and may test aspirate pH, with gastric fluid usually 5 or less.

425.Which findings require action in a chest tube drainage system?

Continuous bubbling in the water seal chamber indicates an air leak, while gentle tidal fluctuation with breathing is expected. Drainage over 100 mL per hour, or a disconnected tube, requires prompt intervention and provider notification.

426.What should the nurse do if a chest tube becomes disconnected from the drainage system?

The nurse immerses the end of the tube 2.5 cm, about 1 inch, in sterile water to create a temporary water seal and then replaces the system. Clamping is avoided because it can cause tension pneumothorax.

427.What nursing care is required before and after a paracentesis?

Have the client void before the procedure to prevent bladder puncture and position upright or sitting on the edge of the bed. Afterward, monitor blood pressure and pulse for hypovolemia, record the volume and character of the fluid, and check the puncture site for leakage.

428.What client teaching is essential for a client with a new pacemaker?

The client avoids raising the arm on the insertion side above the shoulder for about 4 weeks to prevent lead displacement. The client carries a device identification card and takes the pulse as instructed.

429.What are the key nursing responsibilities during hemodialysis through an arteriovenous fistula?

The nurse checks the fistula for a palpable thrill and audible bruit and avoids blood pressure readings or venipuncture in that arm. After dialysis, the nurse monitors for hypotension, bleeding, and disequilibrium syndrome.

430.What signs suggest peritonitis in a client receiving peritoneal dialysis?

Cloudy outflow dialysate, abdominal pain, and fever indicate peritonitis, the most serious complication of peritoneal dialysis. The nurse sends a sample of effluent for culture and notifies the provider.

Physiological Adaptation

431.What is the normal serum potassium range, and which ECG changes signal hyperkalemia?

Normal serum potassium is 3.5 to 5.0 mEq/L. Hyperkalemia produces tall peaked T waves, a widened QRS, and a prolonged PR interval, and it can progress to ventricular fibrillation or asystole, so the nurse places the client on continuous cardiac monitoring.

432.Which assessment findings are typical of Cushing syndrome?

Excess cortisol causes a moon face, buffalo hump, truncal obesity with thin extremities, purple striae, fragile skin, and easy bruising. Clients develop hyperglycemia, hypertension, hypokalemia, sodium and water retention, osteoporosis, and a high risk of infection, so the nurse monitors glucose and protects skin from injury.

433.What is the normal serum sodium range, and what is the main danger of hyponatremia?

Normal serum sodium is 135 to 145 mEq/L. Severe hyponatremia shifts water into brain cells, causing cerebral edema with headache, confusion, seizures, and coma, so the nurse institutes seizure precautions and monitors neurologic status closely.

434.Why must severe chronic hyponatremia be corrected slowly?

Raising sodium too quickly can cause osmotic demyelination syndrome, producing dysarthria, dysphagia, paralysis, and death. Correction is generally limited to no more than 8 to 10 mEq/L in 24 hours, with sodium levels checked frequently during hypertonic saline therapy.

435.Which two bedside signs indicate hypocalcemia?

Chvostek sign is twitching of the facial muscles when the facial nerve is tapped in front of the ear. Trousseau sign is carpal spasm after a blood pressure cuff is inflated above systolic pressure for about three minutes. Both reflect neuromuscular irritability from low calcium.

436.What is the normal total serum calcium, and how does hypercalcemia present?

Normal total serum calcium is about 9.0 to 10.5 mg/dL. Hypercalcemia causes muscle weakness, decreased deep tendon reflexes, constipation, kidney stones, confusion, and a shortened QT interval, and it is common with malignancy and hyperparathyroidism.

437.What is the normal serum magnesium, and which findings suggest hypermagnesemia?

Normal serum magnesium is 1.3 to 2.1 mEq/L. Hypermagnesemia causes flushing, hypotension, bradycardia, loss of deep tendon reflexes, and respiratory depression, and it is most often seen in kidney failure or with magnesium sulfate infusions.

438.How does fluid volume deficit present on assessment?

Hypovolemia presents with tachycardia, a weak thready pulse, orthostatic hypotension, flat neck veins, dry mucous membranes, decreased urine output, and concentrated urine with a specific gravity above 1.030. Hematocrit and BUN rise from hemoconcentration.

439.How can a client prevent dumping syndrome after gastric surgery?

Eat small, frequent meals high in protein and fat and low in simple carbohydrates, and drink fluids between meals rather than with them. Lying down for 20 to 30 minutes after eating slows gastric emptying and reduces cramping, dizziness, tachycardia, and diarrhea.

440.How much fluid does a weight gain of 1 kg represent?

A weight change of 1 kg equals roughly 1 liter of fluid. Daily weights taken at the same time, on the same scale, in similar clothing are the most accurate indicator of fluid balance, and a gain over 2 lb in a day should be reported.

441.Which ABG values indicate respiratory acidosis, and what commonly causes it?

Respiratory acidosis shows a pH below 7.35 with a PaCO2 above 45 mm Hg. It results from hypoventilation, such as COPD exacerbation, opioid overdose, or neuromuscular weakness, and the kidneys compensate by retaining bicarbonate.

442.What are priority interventions for a client in sickle cell vaso-occlusive crisis?

Priorities are prompt IV opioid analgesia, aggressive hydration with IV and oral fluids, and oxygen if the client is hypoxic. Avoid cold compresses, which cause vasoconstriction and more sickling, and monitor for acute chest syndrome, which presents with chest pain, fever, and new pulmonary infiltrates.

443.What breathing pattern compensates for metabolic acidosis in diabetic ketoacidosis?

Kussmaul respirations are deep, rapid breaths that blow off carbon dioxide to raise the pH. The client may also have a fruity acetone breath odor, and the ABG shows a low pH and low bicarbonate with a decreasing PaCO2.

444.Which conditions commonly cause metabolic alkalosis?

Metabolic alkalosis, a pH above 7.45 with bicarbonate above 26 mEq/L, results from prolonged vomiting, continuous nasogastric suction, loop or thiazide diuretics, and excess antacid intake. It is often accompanied by hypokalemia and hypochloremia.

445.What is the normal central venous pressure, and what does a low reading suggest?

Normal central venous pressure is about 2 to 6 mm Hg, or 3 to 8 cm H2O. A low value suggests hypovolemia, while a high value suggests fluid overload or right ventricular failure, and readings are taken with the transducer leveled at the phlebostatic axis.

446.Where is the phlebostatic axis located for hemodynamic monitoring?

The phlebostatic axis lies at the fourth intercostal space along the midaxillary line, approximating the level of the right atrium. The transducer must be leveled and zeroed here for accurate arterial and central venous pressure readings.

447.What mean arterial pressure is needed to perfuse vital organs?

A mean arterial pressure of at least 65 mm Hg is the usual target to maintain perfusion of the brain, kidneys, and heart. MAP is calculated as systolic plus twice the diastolic pressure, divided by three.

448.What are the classic signs of cardiac tamponade?

Beck triad of hypotension, jugular venous distention, and muffled heart sounds signals cardiac tamponade. Pulsus paradoxus, a systolic drop over 10 mm Hg on inspiration, may also occur, and emergency pericardiocentesis is the definitive treatment.

449.How do left-sided and right-sided heart failure differ in presentation?

Left-sided failure backs fluid into the lungs, causing dyspnea, orthopnea, crackles, and pink frothy sputum. Right-sided failure backs fluid into the systemic circulation, causing jugular venous distention, hepatomegaly, ascites, and dependent peripheral edema.

450.Which nursing actions come first for a client with acute pulmonary edema?

Place the client in high Fowler position with legs dependent, apply oxygen, and prepare to give an IV loop diuretic as ordered. Monitor oxygen saturation, lung sounds, and urine output, and anticipate noninvasive positive pressure ventilation if hypoxemia persists.

451.What ECG changes indicate an acute ST-elevation myocardial infarction?

ST elevation of at least 1 mm in two or more contiguous leads indicates STEMI and acute coronary occlusion. The goal is a door-to-balloon time of 90 minutes or less for percutaneous coronary intervention, or fibrinolytics within 30 minutes if PCI is unavailable.

452.Which cardiac biomarker is preferred for diagnosing myocardial infarction?

Cardiac troponin, especially high-sensitivity troponin, is the preferred biomarker because it is highly specific to myocardial injury. It rises within a few hours of injury and remains elevated for 10 to 14 days, so serial levels are drawn.

453.What is the immediate treatment for ventricular fibrillation or pulseless ventricular tachycardia?

Begin high-quality CPR and defibrillate as soon as possible, then resume compressions immediately for two minutes before rechecking rhythm. Epinephrine 1 mg IV is given every 3 to 5 minutes, and amiodarone is added for shock-refractory rhythms.

454.What compression rate and depth does the AHA recommend for adult CPR?

Adult compressions should be delivered at 100 to 120 per minute and at least 2 inches but no more than 2.4 inches deep, allowing full chest recoil. Interruptions should be minimized to under 10 seconds, with a 30 to 2 ratio without an advanced airway.

455.When is synchronized cardioversion used instead of defibrillation?

Synchronized cardioversion treats unstable tachydysrhythmias with a pulse, such as atrial fibrillation or ventricular tachycardia with a pulse. The shock is timed to the R wave to avoid the vulnerable T wave, and the synchronize mode must be reactivated before each shock.

456.What is the main complication risk of atrial fibrillation, and how is it reduced?

Atrial fibrillation causes blood to pool in the atria, raising the risk of thrombus formation and ischemic stroke. Anticoagulation is guided by the CHA2DS2-VASc score, and clients with new onset longer than 48 hours need anticoagulation or imaging before cardioversion.

457.Which findings after cardiac catheterization require immediate action?

Bleeding or a hematoma at the insertion site, loss of distal pulses, a cool pale extremity, or sudden back or flank pain suggesting retroperitoneal bleeding require immediate action. Apply direct pressure for bleeding and notify the provider promptly.

458.What hemodynamic pattern distinguishes cardiogenic shock?

Cardiogenic shock shows low cardiac output with high preload, reflected by elevated pulmonary artery wedge pressure and central venous pressure. The client has hypotension, cool clammy skin, crackles, and oliguria, so fluid boluses are given cautiously or avoided.

459.What are the early warning signs of septic shock?

Early septic shock may present with fever or hypothermia, tachycardia, tachypnea, altered mental status, warm flushed skin, and a lactate of 2 mmol/L or higher. Hypotension requiring vasopressors to keep MAP at least 65 mm Hg defines septic shock.

460.What fluid volume is recommended initially for sepsis-induced hypoperfusion?

Surviving Sepsis guidelines recommend at least 30 mL/kg of IV crystalloid within the first 3 hours for sepsis-induced hypoperfusion or a lactate of 4 mmol/L or more. Blood cultures should be drawn before antibiotics, which are started within one hour.

461.How does anaphylactic shock present, and what is the first-line drug?

Anaphylaxis causes urticaria, angioedema, wheezing, stridor, and hypotension within minutes of exposure. Intramuscular epinephrine in the anterolateral thigh is first-line, 0.5 mg for adults using the 1 mg/mL concentration, repeated every 5 to 15 minutes as needed.

462.What distinguishes neurogenic shock from other forms of shock?

Neurogenic shock follows spinal cord injury at or above T6 and causes hypotension with bradycardia and warm, dry skin from loss of sympathetic tone. Other shock states typically produce tachycardia with cool skin.

463.What are the hallmark findings of acute respiratory distress syndrome?

ARDS presents with refractory hypoxemia that does not improve with increased oxygen, bilateral infiltrates on chest x-ray, and decreased lung compliance. Care includes lung-protective ventilation with low tidal volumes of about 6 mL/kg, PEEP, and prone positioning.

464.What assessment findings suggest a tension pneumothorax?

Tension pneumothorax causes severe dyspnea, absent breath sounds on the affected side, hypotension, jugular venous distention, and tracheal deviation toward the unaffected side. It is a medical emergency treated with immediate needle decompression followed by chest tube insertion.

465.What does continuous bubbling in a chest tube water seal chamber indicate?

Continuous bubbling in the water seal chamber indicates an air leak in the system or the client. Gentle intermittent bubbling with exhalation or coughing is expected, and tidaling, rising and falling with breathing, shows the system is patent.

466.Which assessment findings are characteristic of acute pericarditis?

Acute pericarditis causes sharp chest pain that worsens when lying flat or breathing deeply and improves when sitting up and leaning forward. A pericardial friction rub is the hallmark finding, and the nurse monitors for cardiac tamponade with hypotension, muffled heart sounds, and jugular venous distension.

467.What is the classic presentation of a hip fracture in an older adult?

The affected leg appears shortened, adducted, and externally rotated, with severe pain in the hip or groin and inability to bear weight. Buck traction may be applied briefly before surgery, and the nurse monitors neurovascular status and prevents complications of immobility such as thrombosis and pneumonia.

468.Which findings suggest fat embolism syndrome after a long bone fracture?

Within 12 to 72 hours of a femur or pelvic fracture, the client develops sudden dyspnea, hypoxemia, confusion or restlessness, and a petechial rash over the chest, axillae, and conjunctivae. The nurse gives high-flow oxygen, notifies the provider, and immobilizes fractures early to prevent it.

469.Which mechanical ventilator alarm suggests increased airway pressure?

A high-pressure alarm signals increased resistance, such as secretions, a kinked tube, coughing, biting the tube, or bronchospasm. A low-pressure alarm signals a leak or disconnection, and if the cause cannot be found, the nurse ventilates manually with a bag-valve device.

470.What is the priority if a tracheostomy tube is accidentally dislodged?

Call for help, and if the stoma is mature, insert a new tube with the obturator, which is kept at the bedside along with a spare tube. If unable to replace it, cover the stoma and ventilate by bag-valve mask while help arrives.

471.Which early sign most reliably indicates increased intracranial pressure?

A change in level of consciousness, such as restlessness, confusion, or lethargy, is the earliest and most sensitive sign of increased intracranial pressure. Normal ICP is 5 to 15 mm Hg, and the head of the bed is kept at 30 degrees with the neck midline.

472.What is Cushing triad, and what does it mean?

Cushing triad is widening pulse pressure with rising systolic pressure, bradycardia, and irregular respirations. It is a late sign of severely increased intracranial pressure and impending brain herniation that requires emergency intervention.

473.What is autonomic dysreflexia, and what is the first nursing action?

Autonomic dysreflexia is a hypertensive emergency in clients with spinal cord injury at T6 or above, triggered most often by a full bladder or bowel. Sit the client upright, lower the legs, then find and remove the stimulus, such as a kinked catheter.

474.What is the time window for IV alteplase in acute ischemic stroke?

IV alteplase may be given within 3 hours of symptom onset, extended to 4.5 hours for eligible clients, after CT excludes hemorrhage. Blood pressure must be below 185/110 mm Hg before treatment and kept below 180/105 mm Hg for 24 hours after.

475.What are the priority actions for a client having a tonic-clonic seizure?

Stay with the client, note the time, ease the client to the floor or side-lying position, protect the head, and loosen restrictive clothing. Never insert anything into the mouth or restrain the client, and seizures lasting 5 minutes or longer are treated as status epilepticus.

476.How does the rule of nines estimate burn size in an adult?

The head and each arm are 9 percent of total body surface area, the anterior trunk, posterior trunk, and each leg are 18 percent, and the perineum is 1 percent. This estimate of partial and full thickness burns guides fluid resuscitation and transfer to a burn center.

477.What finding indicates a cerebrospinal fluid leak after head trauma?

Clear drainage from the nose or ears that tests positive for glucose or forms a halo sign, a yellow ring around blood on gauze, suggests a CSF leak. The nurse does not pack the nose and instructs the client to avoid blowing the nose or sneezing forcefully.

478.What is the priority concern during myasthenic crisis?

Myasthenic crisis causes severe muscle weakness that can lead to respiratory failure. The nurse monitors vital capacity and negative inspiratory force, watches for difficulty swallowing and handling secretions, and prepares for intubation and mechanical ventilation.

479.What is the major priority for a client with Guillain-Barre syndrome?

Guillain-Barre syndrome causes ascending symmetric weakness that can reach the respiratory muscles. The priority is monitoring respiratory function, including vital capacity and oxygen saturation, because a falling vital capacity signals the need for mechanical ventilation.

480.Which laboratory findings define diabetic ketoacidosis?

DKA is defined by blood glucose usually above 250 mg/dL, arterial pH below 7.30, bicarbonate below 18 mEq/L, and positive serum or urine ketones. Treatment includes IV fluids, a continuous IV regular insulin infusion, and potassium replacement.

481.Why is potassium monitored closely during insulin treatment of DKA?

Insulin drives potassium back into cells, so serum potassium can fall rapidly and cause dangerous dysrhythmias. Potassium must be at least 3.3 mEq/L before starting insulin, and IV potassium is added to fluids as levels decline.

482.How does hyperosmolar hyperglycemic state differ from DKA?

HHS occurs mainly in type 2 diabetes with glucose often above 600 mg/dL, serum osmolality above 320 mOsm/kg, profound dehydration, and little or no ketosis. Its onset is slower, and neurologic changes and fluid deficits are more severe than in DKA.

483.What is the rule of 15 for treating hypoglycemia in a conscious client?

For blood glucose below 70 mg/dL, give 15 grams of fast-acting carbohydrate, such as 4 ounces of juice, and recheck in 15 minutes. Repeat until glucose is above 70 mg/dL, then provide a snack or meal if the next meal is more than an hour away.

484.What are the signs of thyroid storm?

Thyroid storm causes high fever, severe tachycardia, atrial fibrillation, agitation, delirium, vomiting, and heart failure, often triggered by infection, surgery, or stress. It is life-threatening and requires cooling, beta blockers, antithyroid drugs, and cardiac monitoring.

485.What is myxedema coma, and how does it present?

Myxedema coma is severe decompensated hypothyroidism causing hypothermia, bradycardia, hypoventilation, hyponatremia, hypoglycemia, and decreased consciousness. Treatment includes IV levothyroxine, airway support, and passive rewarming rather than aggressive active warming.

486.What signals adrenal crisis, and what is the emergency treatment?

Adrenal crisis causes profound hypotension, shock, abdominal pain, vomiting, hyponatremia, hyperkalemia, and hypoglycemia, often after abrupt steroid withdrawal or stress. Treatment is IV hydrocortisone and rapid normal saline with dextrose.

487.How do SIADH and diabetes insipidus differ in urine and serum findings?

SIADH causes water retention with concentrated urine, low urine output, and dilutional hyponatremia. Diabetes insipidus causes large volumes of dilute urine with specific gravity below 1.005, high serum osmolality, and hypernatremia.

488.Which signs indicate hypocalcemia after a thyroidectomy?

Accidental injury to the parathyroid glands during thyroidectomy can cause hypocalcemia within 24 to 48 hours. Tingling around the mouth or fingertips, muscle cramps, and positive Chvostek or Trousseau signs are reported, and IV calcium gluconate is kept available.

489.What laboratory values reflect acute kidney injury?

Acute kidney injury is identified by a serum creatinine rise of 0.3 mg/dL within 48 hours or urine output below 0.5 mL/kg per hour for 6 hours. Normal creatinine is about 0.6 to 1.2 mg/dL and BUN is 10 to 20 mg/dL.

490.How should the nurse assess an arteriovenous fistula for dialysis?

Palpate for a thrill and auscultate for a bruit at least every shift to confirm patency. Avoid blood pressure readings, venipuncture, and tight clothing on the access arm, and report an absent thrill or bruit immediately.

491.What symptoms suggest dialysis disequilibrium syndrome?

Dialysis disequilibrium syndrome occurs when urea is removed from the blood faster than from the brain, causing cerebral edema. Headache, nausea, restlessness, confusion, and seizures may appear during or soon after early dialysis sessions, which are kept short.

492.Which laboratory findings indicate disseminated intravascular coagulation?

Disseminated intravascular coagulation shows a low platelet count, low fibrinogen, prolonged PT and aPTT, and elevated D-dimer and fibrin split products. Widespread clotting consumes factors, so the client bleeds from puncture sites, gums, and wounds, and treatment targets the underlying cause with blood product replacement.

493.What are the hallmark signs of peritonitis from a perforated bowel?

Peritonitis causes severe abdominal pain, a rigid boardlike abdomen, rebound tenderness, guarding, fever, and diminished or absent bowel sounds. The client is kept NPO, given IV fluids and antibiotics, and prepared for emergency surgery.

494.What signs indicate hepatic encephalopathy in cirrhosis?

Rising ammonia causes confusion, personality changes, day-night reversal, and asterixis, a flapping tremor of the outstretched hands. Lactulose is given to promote ammonia excretion, with a goal of two to three soft stools daily.

495.What is the priority concern with bleeding esophageal varices?

Ruptured esophageal varices cause massive hematemesis and hypovolemic shock, so airway protection and hemodynamic stability come first. Care includes large-bore IV access, blood transfusion, octreotide infusion, and preparation for endoscopic band ligation.

496.Which findings point to acute pancreatitis?

Acute pancreatitis causes severe epigastric pain radiating to the back, worse after eating and when supine, with nausea and vomiting. Serum lipase rises to at least three times normal, and Cullen or Grey Turner signs suggest hemorrhage.

497.What does sudden relief of pain in suspected appendicitis suggest?

A sudden decrease in pain may signal appendix rupture, followed by worsening diffuse pain, fever, and rigidity from peritonitis. The nurse reports it immediately, avoids heat and laxatives, and keeps the client NPO for surgery.

498.How is the Parkland formula used for burn fluid resuscitation?

The Parkland formula gives 4 mL of lactated Ringer solution times body weight in kg times percent total body surface area burned. Half is given in the first 8 hours from the time of burn and the rest over the next 16 hours.

499.What urine output indicates adequate fluid resuscitation in an adult burn client?

Adequate resuscitation is reflected by urine output of 0.5 mL/kg per hour, or about 30 to 50 mL per hour, in adults. Fluid rates are titrated to this goal rather than given rigidly by formula.

500.What signs of meningeal irritation does the nurse assess in suspected bacterial meningitis?

Classic findings are severe headache, fever, nuchal rigidity, and photophobia. A positive Kernig sign is pain when extending the knee with the hip flexed, and a positive Brudzinski sign is involuntary hip and knee flexion when the neck is flexed.
